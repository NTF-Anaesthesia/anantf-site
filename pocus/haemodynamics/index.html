POCUS · Haemodynamics

Flow and fluid assessment.

Measure flow. Test a response. Check the cost of more fluid.

A vigorous ventricle can deliver little blood. A positive passive leg raise can coexist with pulmonary oedema. This lecture separates those questions.

For adult perioperative and critical care practice. Start after Focused cardiac ultrasound; learn the windows before interpreting the numbers.

  1. Distinguish EF, stroke volume, cardiac output and perfusion.
  2. Acquire LVOT Doppler and recognise measurement error.
  3. Perform and interpret a passive leg raise.
  4. Combine dynamic response with lung and venous findings.

01 · The decision

Three questions before a bolus.

QuestionWhat to establishWhat cannot answer it alone
NeedIs tissue perfusion inadequate, and is fluid an appropriate way to improve it?Hypotension, oliguria or lactate in isolation.
ResponseWill greater preload raise stroke volume or cardiac output?One IVC diameter, CVP or EF.
ToleranceWill more fluid worsen pulmonary or systemic congestion?A positive PLR or a small IVC.

Assess the trajectory: mental state, capillary refill, skin temperature, urine output, lactate, arterial pressure and the suspected mechanism. These variables answer different questions and change at different speeds.

After initial resuscitation, persistent shock should prompt a valid assessment of fluid responsiveness before further fluid. A non-responsive circulation may need vasopressor support, an inotrope or treatment of obstruction; the scan helps identify which mechanism is plausible.

Responsive does not mean thirsty. A stable patient may be responsive without needing fluid. A patient with ARDS may be responsive but poorly tolerant.

02 · Ventricular output

Same EF. Different stroke volume.

EF = (EDV − ESV) / EDV × 100%
Total LV stroke volume = EDV × EF / 100

At EF 70%, an EDV of 40 mL gives a total stroke volume of only 28 mL. A larger chamber can eject more blood despite a lower EF.

With mitral regurgitation, part of LV ejection returns to the left atrium. A preserved total EF can therefore coexist with reduced forward stroke volume. The model holds EF and EDV constant while you vary the proportion returning through the mitral valve.

Isolated MR model: forward SV = total SV × (1 − regurgitant fraction). It assumes no other regurgitation or shunt. It does not model the loading changes caused by real MR or determine MR severity.

Schematic · volume and ejection

Try EDV 40 mL and EF 70%, then restore EDV 120 mL and add MR. Describe total and forward ejection separately.

03 · Acquisition

Match the area to the velocity.

  1. Diameter: zoom the LVOT in an on-axis PLAX view. Measure inner edge to inner edge in midsystole, following the laboratory protocol; the 2019 ASE method places this approximately 3–10 mm proximal to the valve plane.
  2. Doppler: use A5C or A3C and align as parallel to LVOT flow as possible. Place the PW sample in the outflow tract, initially about 5 mm proximal to the aortic valve.
  3. Optimise: obtain a clear systolic spectrum. Broadening or valve contamination suggests the gate needs repositioning away from the acceleration region.
  4. Trace: follow the dense modal signal, excluding faint spectral noise. Average representative beats and document the rhythm.

The diameter and Doppler sample must represent the same anatomical level. A precise trace cannot rescue an oblique diameter.

Use colour to find the flow direction, then refine the view. Routine electronic angle correction is not recommended for cardiac Doppler; improve alignment by moving the probe.

Keep the view, gate, gain and tracing method consistent for serial comparisons. Avoid changing vasoactive support or ventilation during a diagnostic manoeuvre unless clinical safety requires it.

ASE comprehensive TTE, 2019

04 · From distance to flow

VTI is a distance. Area makes it a volume.

Area = π × (diameter / 2)²
SV = area × VTI
CO = SV × heart rate / 1000

Diameter and VTI in centimetres give stroke volume in millilitres. Multiplying by beats per minute and dividing by 1000 gives litres per minute.

For diameter 2.0 cm, VTI 20 cm and HR 80/min: area 3.14 cm², stroke volume 62.8 mL and cardiac output 5.03 L/min. Adequacy still depends on body size, metabolic demand and perfusion.

Significant aortic regurgitation can make systolic LVOT flow exceed net systemic flow. Shunts, obstruction and irregular rhythm also need a more complete assessment.

Inputs represent an ideal reference signal. Angle and diameter-error controls introduce isolated measurement errors; real acquisition errors may coexist.

Schematic · LVOT area and Doppler envelope

Set diameter error to +10%. Then change only the Doppler angle. Explain why the errors can partly cancel without making the measurement reliable.

05 · Precision

Small errors can change the conclusion.

ChangeEffect in the ideal modelPractical response
Diameter overestimated by 10%Area and calculated SV increase by 21%.Review the view and calipers before trusting absolute output.
Diameter underestimated by 10%Area and calculated SV decrease by 19%.The squared relationship is not symmetrical.
Doppler angle increasesMeasured velocity falls approximately with cos θ.Improve alignment; do not average poor and good windows.
HR changes during a testVTI change and CO change diverge.Record HR and interpret the complete flow response.

For a short intervention with stable LVOT area, relative VTI change estimates relative stroke-volume change. Re-measuring diameter at every stage adds noise. A baseline of 16 cm and test value of 18 cm gives a 12.5% rise; the same two values with an unstable gate do not establish a physiological response.

Repeat baseline traces when a result is close to the decision threshold. Reproducibility, not the calculator’s decimal places, determines confidence.

06 · Reversible preload challenge

Perform the manoeuvre before interpreting its number.

  1. Start semi-recumbent with a stable flow baseline.
  2. Use the bed to lower the trunk while raising the legs to about 45°. This recruits blood from both the legs and splanchnic compartment.
  3. Measure the early maximal SV or CO response, usually within the first minute. The effect can fade quickly.
  4. Return to the original position and confirm that flow returns towards baseline.

A blood-pressure change alone is an inadequate substitute for the flow response. VTI tracks SV if the LVOT area and acquisition remain stable.

PLR can be useful during spontaneous breathing or arrhythmia, when respiratory variation indices are unreliable. The flow measurement must still be sufficiently precise.

Avoid pain, coughing, active leg lifting and sympathetic stimulation. A new tachycardia is a clue to a confounded test. Increased intra-abdominal pressure can cause a false negative; clinical constraints may make positioning unsuitable.

Do not delay urgent resuscitation to complete a test in a deteriorating patient.

Monnet & Teboul, 2015

07 · Response over time

The peak can disappear before a late measurement.

Run each scripted scenario. Compare baseline, the early PLR peak, and recovery after the bed returns to its starting position.

  • Responsive: a transient flow rise with little HR change.
  • No meaningful rise: preload increases without a clear SV response.
  • Tachycardia: a rising CO may reflect sympathetic stimulation rather than the preload effect being tested.

Change (%) = (during − baseline) / baseline × 100

The denominator is baseline, not the mean of the two values. A result is interpretable only if the test was performed correctly and the signal remained stable.

08 · Interpreting a change

A borderline number needs better measurement.

Around 10% is a commonly studied PLR-induced CO threshold. One TTE study in spontaneously breathing patients used a ≥12.5% SV rise to predict a ≥15% response to fluid. These are method- and protocol-specific results, not interchangeable universal cut-offs.

A study of repeated TTE examinations found a median least significant VTI change of 11% between examinations by the same operator. Precision was better when the probe remained in place. Averaging three beats in sinus rhythm and five in AF gave adequate within-examination precision in that study; more sampling may be needed with marked variability.

If VTI rises by 9–12%, ask: was alignment unchanged, was the peak captured, were representative beats averaged, did HR change, and did the value return to baseline?

Repeat an uncertain test or use an alternative valid method. Do not turn a small noisy change into a confident instruction to give or withhold fluid.

Lamia et al., 2007 · Jozwiak et al., 2019

09 · Caval variation

Name the formula before quoting the percentage.

Follow the IVC into the right atrium. Measure perpendicular to the vessel, and observe several breaths. Off-centre cuts and translation out of the image plane can imitate collapse.

Collapsibility = ΔD / Dmax × 100
Distensibility = ΔD / Dmin × 100
Variability = ΔD / Dmean × 100

Here Dmean is (Dmax + Dmin)/2. With 20 and 10 mm, the three indices are 50%, 100% and 66.7%. They describe the same diameters using different denominators.

Historical controlled-ventilation studies used thresholds such as 18% distensibility and 12% variability. Their settings and patient selection matter; neither is a universal prescription.

Schematic · calibre, breathing and indices

Spontaneous inspiration usually narrows the IVC; passive positive-pressure inspiration may distend it. The model isolates this usual pattern and does not diagnose volume status.

10 · Heart–lung interaction

The ventilator changes preload and afterload.

Positive intrathoracic pressure can reduce venous return. Lung inflation and pulmonary vascular effects can also increase RV afterload; the response depends on lung volume, compliance and existing RV disease.

A large IVC on PEEP does not prove excess circulating volume. A small IVC with vigorous inspiratory effort does not prove that fluid is safe. Interpret the vessel alongside RV function, ventilation and perfusion.

Record whether breathing is spontaneous, assisted or fully controlled; include tidal volume, PEEP, rhythm and any relevant abdominal pressure.

When respiratory indices mislead

  • Spontaneous effort or patient–ventilator interaction.
  • Low tidal volume or low respiratory-system compliance.
  • Arrhythmia, especially for arterial pulse-pressure variation.
  • RV dysfunction or altered pulmonary vascular load.
  • Raised intra-abdominal pressure.

End-expiratory occlusion, tidal-volume challenges and mini-fluid challenges require defined protocols and precise flow monitoring. Do not increase tidal volume simply to obtain a more convenient number.

11 · The cost of volume

Look downstream as well as forward.

DomainUseful observationsInterpretation
LungB-line distribution, new confluent artefacts, consolidation and effusions.Reduced aeration has several causes. Serial change may warn that fluid is worsening pulmonary oedema.
Right heartRV size, function, septal interaction, tricuspid regurgitation.A distended failing RV may worsen with further volume.
Systemic veinsIVC plus hepatic, portal and intrarenal Doppler.Venous abnormalities support congestion; identify its cause.
Clinical trajectoryOxygenation, work of breathing, organ function, fluid balance and perfusion.Integrate with ultrasound rather than replacing clinical assessment.

A-lines do not grant permission to infuse. B-lines do not prove cardiogenic oedema. New adverse pulmonary or venous findings during resuscitation should trigger reassessment before further fluid.

If fluid is appropriate, define its purpose, amount, delivery interval and reassessment endpoint beforehand. Reassess SV or CO and tissue perfusion alongside signs of harm.

12 · Venous Doppler

A congestion score is not a treatment order.

VExUS combines IVC size with hepatic, portal and intrarenal venous waveforms. In the original scheme, IVC <2 cm gives grade 0. With IVC ≥2 cm: no severe waveform gives grade 1; one gives grade 2; two or more give grade 3.

Severe patterns are hepatic systolic flow reversal, portal pulsatility ≥50%, and discontinuous monophasic intrarenal venous flow. Time waveforms against the cardiac cycle and confirm that the signal is venous.

Portal pulsatility = (Vmax − Vmin) / Vmax × 100%

Practise waveform recognition in the cardiac lecture →

What the score cannot establish

  • Grade 0 means no congestion by this scheme; it does not guarantee fluid tolerance.
  • High grades do not distinguish fluid overload from RV failure, severe TR or obstructive physiology.
  • Severe TR alters hepatic Doppler; portal pulsatility can occur in healthy people. Renal signals can be technically difficult.
  • The original study linked severe congestion to AKI after cardiac surgery. An association does not prove that treating to a score improves outcomes.

Use the finding to reassess further fluid and seek the mechanism. Decongestion is a considered treatment for congestion, not an automatic response to an isolated score.

Beaubien-Souligny et al., 2020

13 · Case

The PLR is positive. The lungs are deteriorating.

A patient with septic shock has persistent poor capillary refill despite initial resuscitation. A technically stable PLR raises VTI from 16 to 19 cm, with unchanged HR and recovery to baseline. Oxygen requirement has risen, with new bilateral confluent B-lines and an impaired RV.

Does this establish that the next treatment should be fluid?

Reveal reasoning

The rise is 18.75%, supporting preload responsiveness under these test conditions. It does not establish tolerance or prove that more fluid is the best way to improve perfusion.

Reassess the pulmonary process, RV function, systemic congestion and vasoactive support. The new findings weigh against an automatic bolus. Treat the mechanism and monitor both perfusion and harm; a responsive patient can still have a poor risk–benefit balance for further volume.

Constructed teaching vignette. The combination of findings is deliberate; it is not a validated decision rule.

14 · Case

A convincing percentage from an unstable trace.

VTI changes from 16 to 18 cm during PLR. The operator moves the probe to regain the window, the patient coughs, and HR rises from 80 to 105/min. No recovery measurement is obtained.

What should be repeated before calling this responsive?

Reveal reasoning

The arithmetic is correct: +12.5%. The inference is not secure. Changed alignment or sample position, coughing and tachycardia confound the result.

Re-establish a stable baseline, explain the manoeuvre, retain the same window where possible, sample representative beats, capture the early peak and confirm recovery. If a reliable echo signal cannot be maintained, choose another validated flow method suited to the clinical circumstances.

VTI is a stroke-volume surrogate. With a changing HR it is not interchangeable with cardiac-output change.

15 · Case

The IVC is large. What has caused it?

Following a PEEP increase, a ventilated patient becomes hypotensive. The IVC measures 24 mm with little variation. The RV is larger than on the previous scan, and the septum is flattened in systole.

Would the IVC alone justify diuresis or another fluid bolus?

Reveal reasoning

Neither. The findings raise concern for adverse heart–lung interaction and RV loading. Reassess ventilation, oxygenation, perfusion, RV function and alternative causes, including pneumothorax or pulmonary vascular obstruction when clinically plausible.

Do not apply the spontaneous-breathing IVC-to-RAP table as though PEEP had no effect. A pressure-related venous change is not a direct measurement of total circulating volume.

Review the intervention with the treating team and repeat the haemodynamic assessment. Treat the cause rather than a diameter.
