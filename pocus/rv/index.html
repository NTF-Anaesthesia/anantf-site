RV · Anatomy and physiology

The RV wraps around the LV.

Short axis: a crescent around the round LV. Apical: a triangle. Dilated: rounder, and can take over the apex (normally LV).

  • Two parts: inflow (sinus) and outflow (conus, infundibulum).
  • Bellows-like contraction, a wave from inflow → outflow; free wall runs apex → outflow tract.
  • Ejection: longitudinal shortening + inward free-wall movement + septal motion.
Built for a low-pressure circuit
  • Pulmonary pressures ≈ one-sixth of systemic → thin wall.
  • Very afterload-sensitive: a sudden rise (PE, hypoxia, acidosis, high airway pressure) → dilatation and failure. Tolerates volume far better than pressure.
  • Shared septum: the LV normally indents the RV and LV contraction helps RV ejection. Each ventricle affects the other.
Why this matters at induction

RV dilates → septum shifts towards LV → LV filling and cardiac output ↓ → systemic pressure ↓ → RV coronary perfusion ↓ → RV worse. Positive-pressure ventilation ↑ RV afterload. Big, poorly contracting RV: support BP first, no reflex fluid boluses, senior help before induction.

Which ventricle is which

  • Tricuspid annulus sits slightly nearer the apex than the mitral.
  • Moderator band crosses the RV near its apex.
  • LV normally forms the apex.

RV-focused apical view

  1. Standard A4C.
  2. Rock towards the RV to centre it.
  3. Rotate to the widest RV with whole free wall and tricuspid annulus; keep the LV apex in view.
  4. Don't clip the lateral wall or cut obliquely: off-axis undersizes the RV.
How a standard A4C can mislead

LV-centred views may show only part of the RV; rotation changes apparent size and area. For serial measurements use the same RV-focused plane at comparable loading.

RV · Function

Eyeball first. FAC and TAPSE measure different things.

Eyeball: does the free wall move in towards the septum, and the tricuspid annulus towards the apex? Then measure: FAC → TAPSE → RV output (RVOT VTI; beyond FoCUS, not covered).

Fractional area change

Complete RV-focused A4C. Trace endocardium at end-diastole and end-systole (trabeculations inside the cavity): annulus → free wall → apex → septum. Whole contour visible.

FAC = (EDA − ESA) / EDA × 100%

Normal >35% (ASE 2025). Projected area only: omits the RVOT, sensitive to tracing and plane, not a direct RVEF.

TAPSE

Apical view; M-mode cursor aligned with lateral tricuspid annular motion. Measure base-to-peak systolic excursion (not a chamber length or tissue-Doppler velocity).

Normal >17 mm (ASE 2025). Angle- and load-dependent; mainly basal longitudinal function. Translation and tethering can preserve it despite poor global function.

Older TAPSE cut-offs

2020 slides: TAPSE ≤16 mm (or <16 mm) highly suggestive of dysfunction; FAC <35% dysfunction. Here: ASE 2025 (TAPSE >17 mm, FAC >35% normal). Near the cut-off, integrate FAC, size, free-wall motion, septal shape and clinical state.

Real examples

RV · Septal interaction

When does the septum go flat?

True PSAX, papillary level: the LV stays round all cycle. A loaded RV pushes the septum in → D-shaped LV. Timing tells the load.

Septum flat inSuggestsFor example
SystoleRV pressure overloadPE, pulmonary hypertension, ARDS or high airway pressure
Diastole onlyRV volume overloadSevere tricuspid regurgitation, atrial septal defect
Systole and diastoleCombined pressure and volume overloadPulmonary hypertension with significant TR

Severe pressure overload alone can keep it flat into early diastole: judge end-systole carefully.

Timing

  • Freeze and step frame by frame; use the ECG.
  • End-diastole: QRS, mitral valve closing.
  • End-systole: smallest LV, just before mitral opening.
  • Check the plane: an oblique cut fakes a D.

Practise in the RV patterns lab: Chronic PH or RV volume overload, freeze systole and diastole.

Question 1

What does this clip show?

Answer: RV segmental wall-motion abnormality consistent with RV infarction.

  • RCA supplies most of the RV → RV infarction usually with inferior LV involvement. Here distal RV and LV are both affected.
  • Labelled subcostal but looks parasternal: name the window from what you see. Regional abnormality ≠ globally dilated, hypokinetic RV.
  • Often preload-dependent: caution with venodilators and positive-pressure ventilation.

Question 2

42-year-old woman with known pulmonary hypertension, shocked after aspiration in theatre. What does this clip show?

Answer: dilated RV with moderate to severe dysfunction.

  • Aspiration and hypoxia ↑ PVR; a chronically loaded RV has little reserve.
  • Treated with inhaled nitric oxide: PVR ↓ → RV unloads → septum moves back → filling improves.

84-year-old man with urosepsis on noradrenaline for MAP and nasal-cannula oxygen. What do these clips show?

Answer: severely dilated RV and severe RV dysfunction.

  • A4C: thrombus at the RV apex along the free wall.
  • Leg ultrasound: DVT; V/Q high probability. Thrombolysed; survived acute PE.
  • "Septic" shock can be obstructive. New big RV → scan the legs (DVT compression ultrasound).

The right ventricle

Describe it before diagnosing it.

  1. Several windows, including an RV-focused apical view.
  2. Report size, free-wall thickness, contraction, septal timing.
  3. Read FAC and TAPSE with the whole exam and loading conditions.
  4. RV dilatation or McConnell sign alone neither confirms nor excludes PE.
  5. Clinical context and serial change guide what to investigate next.
