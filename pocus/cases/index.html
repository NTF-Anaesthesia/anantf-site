Case 1 · IVC technique · course slides 2–5

Hypotension in ICU. Is the IVC telling the truth?

1 · The patient

A patient becomes hypotensive soon after ICU admission. The team wants to use the inferior vena cava (IVC) as a guide to volume status, and this is the clip they show you.

2 · The clip

3 · Commit: which of these is true?

4 · Reveal

  • The IVC moves in and out of the image plane. When the beam cuts the edge of a tube instead of its centre, the tube looks narrower, so movement can look like collapse.
  • Fix the picture first. Keep the IVC in long axis, show it entering the right atrium, and find the hepatic vein joining it. Rock and sweep until you have the widest diameter, then hold that plane through the whole breath.
  • The course measures the diameter 1–2 cm from the junction of the right atrium and IVC, at end-expiration (Lang 2015, ASE/EACVI chamber quantification).

What the right plane looks like

Teaching point

It is not a sin to get a wobbly IVC view. It is a sin to treat a patient on one. Check the acquisition before you interpret the physiology. Even a technically good IVC only answers a narrow question; size and variation depend on breathing effort, ventilation and the right heart.

Case 2 · LV function · course slides 6–11

Failing to wean from NIV.

1 · The patient

A 74-year-old man with a presumed history of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) develops respiratory distress and is started on non-invasive ventilation (NIV). He fails several attempts at weaning.

2 · Commit: what is the most appropriate next step?

Reveal

  • The COPD is only presumed. Repeated weaning failure is a reason to look for a cause you have not proven or excluded, and the heart is the obvious one.
  • Taking away positive pressure raises venous return and left ventricular afterload. A ventricle that coped on NIV may fail when the support comes off, and the patient becomes breathless again with pulmonary oedema.
  • Repeating a test that has not helped, or simply waiting, does not change the plan.

3 · The echo

4 · Commit: the cause of NIV failure is congestive heart failure. What is the ejection fraction?

Reveal

  • All three views show severe global LV dysfunction: little wall thickening and a cavity that barely changes size. Across three windows that is enough to call the EF severely reduced by eye.
  • You do not need Simpson’s biplane to answer this bedside question. Grade by eye in more than one view, and measure when the answer would change management or when you are tracking change.
  • What happened: a negative fluid balance was achieved and he was weaned off NIV.

The course slide also summarises Doppler indices of diastolic function in weaning (mitral inflow and tissue Doppler, from Mayo et al. 2016) as an introduction to advanced echo; the course notes ask learners to point out the EF only. Those thresholds are not reproduced here because they were not re-checked for this page.

Teaching point

When a patient keeps failing to come off positive pressure, scan the heart before you blame the lungs. A poor LV turns weaning into a cardiac stress test. Treat the cause (here, fluid off), then try again.

Case 3 · Masses and effusions · course slides 12–14

Lung cancer and shock on three vasopressors.

1 · The patient

A man with a recent diagnosis of lung cancer presents in shock needing three vasopressors. You do a bedside echo.

2 · The clip

3 · Commit: what is the most likely diagnosis?

4 · Reveal

  • A large echogenic mass sits in the left atrium, and there is pericardial fluid around the heart.
  • What happened: thoracotomy showed the lung cancer extending into the left atrium through the pulmonary veins.
  • Ultrasound describes; it does not give histology. Where the mass is, what it attaches to, how it moves and the clinical story narrow the list. In a patient with a new cancer, think of direct spread or metastasis as well as thrombus.

Teaching point

Find the finding, then ask how it explains the shock. Is the effusion compressing a chamber? Is the mass blocking filling of the left ventricle? Say what you see, say what you cannot tell, and get definitive imaging and the right team early.

Case 4 · RV against LV · course slides 15–18

Sepsis, a high CVP and low urine output.

1 · The patient

A patient with sepsis has a point-of-care echo as part of the work-up. Urine output is low.

  • HR 140/min
  • BP 135/65 mm Hg
  • CVP 18 mm Hg
  • Urine output low

2 · The clip

3 · Commit: which right ventricular finding does this show?

4 · Reveal

  • The right ventricle looks vigorous because the left ventricle next to it is hypokinetic. Read the ventricles against each other, not one in isolation.
  • This is not hypovolaemia: a CVP of 18 mm Hg and a poorly contracting LV do not fit an empty circulation.
  • It is not McConnell sign either. McConnell sign is akinesia of the RV free wall with a normally moving RV apex; it describes the RV itself, not how it compares with the LV.
  • What happened: he received aggressive fluid resuscitation and developed respiratory distress. Work-up found left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery disease.

Teaching point

Low urine output in sepsis is not an order for more fluid. A high CVP, a poor LV and a congested patient say the tank is not the problem. Before the next bolus, ask whether the patient needs it, will respond to it and will tolerate it.

Case 5 · Regional wall motion · course slides 19–21

VF on day 1 after aortic root replacement.

1 · The patient

A 73-year-old man had an aortic root replacement for a severe chronic type A dissection of the aortic root. On postoperative day 1 he goes into ventricular fibrillation (VF).

2 · The clips: before and after the arrest

Contrast echo (an ultrasound enhancing agent, Optison) fills the LV cavity so the endocardial border is easier to see. Compare each wall before and after.

3 · Commit: what is the most appropriate next step?

4 · Reveal

  • After the arrest there is severe hypokinesis to akinesis of the anterior wall, apex and septum, while most of the lateral wall (circumflex territory) and the inferior wall (right coronary territory) move normally. That pattern fits the LAD territory.
  • After VT or VF arrest, a segmental wall motion abnormality after return of circulation is a surrogate for coronary artery disease and should prompt referral for cardiac catheterisation (Levitov 2016, cited by the course).
  • Ventricular arrhythmia after cardiac surgery should make you think of ischaemia. A root replacement also involves reattaching the coronary arteries, so a new territorial abnormality needs a coronary answer.
  • Resternotomy, mechanical support or fluid treat other problems; none of them answers the question this scan raises. The decision belongs with the cardiac surgical and cardiology teams, and your scan should reach them early.

Teaching point

Scan early after an arrhythmia or arrest, and compare with the baseline if there is one. A wall motion abnormality that follows a coronary territory changes the next step.

Case 6 · Source of sepsis · course slides 22–25

A septic 18-year-old. Where is the source?

1 · The patient

An 18-year-old man has a sore throat, sweats, breathlessness and diarrhoea.

  • Temperature 40 °C
  • BP 87/42 mm Hg
  • Lactate 2.7 (units not stated on the course slide)
2 · Commit: which ultrasound examination is most likely to reveal the source of his sepsis?

Reveal

The course scanned all four. Look at each before you read the labels below the grid.

3 · The four-view survey

Answer: venous ultrasound

  • Diagnosis: Fusobacterium necrophorum bacteraemia with Lemierre syndrome, a septic thrombophlebitis after a throat infection. Classically it involves the internal jugular vein; the course labels the view only as right neck.
  • The sore throat was the clue. The heart was normal, the B-lines were non-specific and the abdomen was clear: a whole-body survey is most useful when the history tells you where to look hardest.

Teaching point

Ultrasound in sepsis is not only the heart and lungs. Let the history pick the window, and remember that B-lines show fluid in the lung, not its cause.

Case 7 · Post-cardiac surgery · course slides 26–28

Hypotension after pacing-wire removal.

1 · The patient

A 67-year-old man had coronary artery bypass grafting. His postoperative course needed an intra-aortic balloon pump and an open chest. Three weeks later, before transfer to the ward, his epicardial pacing wires are removed and he becomes hypotensive.

2 · The clip

3 · Commit: what is the most appropriate next step?

4 · Reveal

  • Pericardial tamponade after pacing-wire removal. The course’s file label for this clip is “RV haematoma post wire removal”: a localised collection compressing the right ventricle.
  • After cardiac surgery, blood around the heart is often clotted and localised rather than a free circumferential effusion, so a needle is unlikely to drain it. Surgical re-exploration treats both the collection and the bleeding.
  • Fluid and inotropes may buy minutes while the team assembles; they do not fix the problem.
  • Transthoracic echo can give useful clues after cardiac surgery, but windows are often poor. A limited scan without a large effusion does not exclude tamponade. TOE may be needed if TTE is inadequate.

Teaching point

New hypotension after pacing-wire removal is tamponade until proven otherwise. Call the surgeon early; do not wait for a textbook image.
