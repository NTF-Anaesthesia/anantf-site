POCUS/Focused cardiac ultrasound

Critical care POCUS · Station 1 & 5

Focused cardiac ultrasound.

Five views, five questions. Get the picture, then judge the pump.

Five views. Five questions. One bedside decision.

Focused cardiac ultrasound (FoCUS) is a goal-directed, qualitative scan. The clinician at the bedside does it to answer a small number of time-critical, mostly yes/no questions in a shocked or breathless patient. It is not a comprehensive echocardiogram, and a normal FoCUS does not rule out structural heart disease.

By the end of this station you should be able to

  • Hold a phased-array probe in the cardiac convention and get PLAX, PSAX, A4C, subcostal 4-chamber and IVC views.
  • Recognise the most common faults in each view and correct them.
  • Grade LV systolic function by eye, backed up by EPSS, fractional shortening and wall thickening.
  • Call a dilated, failing RV and tell acute from chronic.
  • Tell pericardial from pleural effusion and epicardial fat, and recognise tamponade physiology.
  • Use the IVC, LVOT VTI and passive leg raise sensibly, and know their limits.
  • Grade venous congestion with VExUS and use it to decide when to stop fluid.
  • Know when to stop and ask for a formal echo.

01 · The FoCUS questions

Ask a question you can answer. Then scan to answer it.

The 4A framework: same order every time.

  • 1
    AskWhat is the clinical question, and would the answer change what I do now? If not, don't scan. Treat.
  • 2
    AcquireAim for at least two windows that answer the question. In a supine, ventilated patient, subcostal plus a parasternal view is often the most practical pair.
  • 3
    AssessAnswer the five core questions, each with yes / no / can't tell.
  • 4
    ActFit the answer into the clinical picture, treat, and rescan after the intervention.

Rules
  • Answer each question from at least two views if you can. One view can mislead.
  • Can't tell is an honest answer. A poor-quality scan is not a negative scan.
  • FoCUS is repeatable. Rescan after a fluid bolus, a vasopressor or intubation.
  • SCCM 2024 focused update: ultrasound is suggested to guide management in septic shock, acute dyspnoea or respiratory failure and cardiogenic shock, and for targeted volume management (Díaz-Gómez 2025).
  • Write down which views you got, how good they were, and your answers.

02 · Probe and knobology

Phased array, cardiac preset, marker on the right.

Probe: phased array (sector), about 1–5 MHz. Its small footprint fits between ribs, and it can image to depth.

Orientation conventions
  • Cardiac preset: the screen marker is on the right of the screen (echocardiography convention).
  • Abdominal / radiology preset: the screen marker is on the left.
  • If you scan the heart with an abdominal preset, the image comes out mirrored left to right. Either switch to the cardiac preset, or rotate the probe 180° from the positions on this page. Don't do both.
  • On this page, all probe-marker directions assume the cardiac preset: PLAX to the right shoulder (about 10 o'clock); PSAX to the left shoulder (about 2 o'clock); A4C and subcostal 4C to the patient's left (3 o'clock); IVC to the head (12 o'clock).
Knobs that matter
  • Depth: start deep (about 16–20 cm) so you can see the descending aorta and any pleural fluid behind the heart. Then reduce depth until the heart fills about two-thirds of the sector.
  • Gain: blood in the cavities should be black. Too much gain fills the cavities with speckle and can mimic thrombus or smoke. Too little loses endocardial borders and the edges of an effusion.
  • Focus / sector width: narrow the sector to raise the frame rate when you are judging motion.
  • Cine loop: always review the last few beats. Freeze to measure, then scroll.
  • ECG lead: if the machine has one, attach it. Tamponade timing and diastolic measurements depend on it.
Hold and move

Pencil grip, with the heel of the hand resting on the chest to stop drift. Use small movements. The four movements, named the same way all station: slide (move along the skin), rotate (turn about the probe's long axis), tilt (rock towards or away from the marker, in the imaging plane), fan (angle across the imaging plane).

03 · Five windows

Three places on the chest, five views.

Parasternal window

Left sternal edge, 3rd–5th intercostal space; left lateral decubitus if possible.

  • PLAX: marker to the right shoulder.
  • PSAX: from PLAX, rotate 90° clockwise so the marker points to the left shoulder.
Apical window

Point of maximal impulse, usually 5th intercostal space at about the anterior axillary line; further lateral in a big heart or in left lateral decubitus.

  • A4C: marker to the patient's left, beam aimed at the right shoulder.
Subcostal window

Just below the xiphoid; supine; knees flexed relaxes the abdomen.

  • Subcostal 4-chamber: probe almost flat on the abdomen, marker to the patient's left, aimed at the left shoulder, looking through the liver.
  • IVC long axis: from the subcostal 4C, centre the RA, then rotate anticlockwise until the marker points to the head (12 o'clock). Follow the IVC into the RA.
When the window is poor
  • Ventilated, COPD or obese patients: parasternal and apical windows are often poor. The subcostal window is often the best and is reachable without moving the patient.
  • Post-laparotomy, distended or painful abdomen: subcostal is limited, so go parasternal.
  • Breath-hold at end-expiration, or a brief end-expiratory pause on the ventilator, if safe.

    04 · Parasternal long axis

    PLAX: the LV lies flat, and the aorta tells you where you are.

    What you should see, near field to far field

    Chest wall → RV / RVOT → interventricular septum → LV cavity → posterior LV wall → pericardium (bright line) → descending thoracic aorta in cross-section, behind the LA. On screen-right: the aortic root with the aortic valve, and the LA beneath it. The anterior mitral leaflet is continuous with the posterior aortic root.

    What it answers
    • LV systolic function: eyeball, EPSS, fractional shortening.
    • Pericardial versus pleural effusion. The descending aorta is the landmark.
    • Gross aortic and mitral valve red flags.
    • RVOT size: compare the RVOT with the aortic root. In normal hearts they are similar.

    05 · Parasternal short axis

    PSAX: a round LV at papillary level, or it isn't short axis.

    How to get it: from a good PLAX, keep the LV in the centre and rotate 90° clockwise (marker to the left shoulder). Tilt the beam towards the apex (towards the patient's left hip), i.e. move the probe tail towards the right shoulder, until both papillary muscles appear.

    Levels, base to apex (tilt to move between them)

    Aortic valve level (the 'Mercedes sign', with RVOT and pulmonary valve) → mitral valve level (the 'fish mouth') → mid-papillary level, the FoCUS standard → apical level.

    What it answers
    • Global LV function: concentric inward motion and thickening. The best single view for 'how is it squeezing'.
    • Regional wall motion at mid-papillary level: LAD anterior and anteroseptal; RCA inferior and inferoseptal; LCx inferolateral and anterolateral. Territories overlap; this is approximate.
    • The septum: a round 'O' LV is normal. A D-shaped LV means RV overload: flattening in systole and diastole means pressure overload; in diastole only means volume overload.
    • Hyperdynamic, empty LV: papillary muscles touch at end-systole ('kissing papillary muscles').
    ErrorFix
    LV oval or egg-shapedOff-axis: rotate a few degrees either way until round
    Fish mouth (mitral valve) instead of papillary musclesTilt to aim further towards the apex
    Pseudo-D-shapeAn oblique cut flattens the septum: correct the rotation before calling a D-sign
    06 · Apical four-chamber

    A4C: the apex goes at the top, sharp, not round.

    How to get it: left lateral decubitus with the left arm up if possible. Find the apex beat. Place the probe there, marker to the patient's left (3 o'clock), and aim towards the right shoulder. Slide until the apex is at the top-centre of the screen and the septum is vertical.

    What you should see

    LV on screen-right, RV on screen-left, atria below. Mitral and tricuspid valves are visible. The tricuspid annulus sits slightly closer to the apex than the mitral annulus.

    What it answers
    • RV:LV size comparison: the key view for RV size.
    • TAPSE: M-mode through the lateral tricuspid annulus.
    • Global and regional LV function (septal and lateral walls).
    • Pericardial effusion: lateral to the LV, and around the RA.
    • Gross valve red flags.
    • LVOT VTI: tilt anteriorly to get the apical 5-chamber view.

    07 · Subcostal and IVC

    Subcostal: the window that works when the others don't.

    Subcostal 4-chamber
    • Supine, knees flexed. Probe just below the xiphoid, almost flat (10–30° to the skin), marker to the patient's left. Aim at the left shoulder through the liver.
    • On screen: liver at the top, then RV free wall, RV, septum, LV. In the cardiac preset the atria lie on the left of the screen and the apex points to the right.
    • Press firmly and wait for the patient to exhale. Don't fight the liver: use it as the window.
    • Best view for: pericardial effusion (between the liver and the RV free wall), RV free-wall diastolic collapse, cardiac arrest pulse checks, ventilated or COPD patients.
    IVC long axis
    • From subcostal 4C, centre the RA. Rotate anticlockwise until the marker points to the head (12 o'clock). Fan slightly to the patient's right.
    • Confirm it is the IVC: it enters the RA; a hepatic vein drains into it; it has a thin wall; its size changes with respiration.
    • The aorta lies more to the left and deeper. It has thick walls, does not enter the RA, and pulsates with arterial timing.
    • Measure perpendicular to the IVC long axis, about 1–2 cm from the cavo-atrial junction, just caudal to where the hepatic vein joins, at end-expiration. M-mode can help but beware of translational movement.

    Only liver seen, heart deep or absentFlatten the probe, push deeper, increase depth, bend knees
    Narrow IVC that 'collapses' oddlyCylinder effect: the beam is cutting the IVC off-centre. Fan side to side until it is at its widest; check in short axis

    08 · Troubleshooting

    Name the fault. Fix it in one move.

    Most bad images come from a small number of faults. Learn to name each one and make a single correction rather than wandering.

    PLAXLV sloping → change rib space. No aortic valve → rotate towards the right shoulder.
    PSAXOval → rotate. Fish mouth → tilt to the apex.
    A4CForeshortened → slide laterally / down a space. 5-chamber → tilt posteriorly.
    SubcostalNo heart → flatten, deepen, bend knees.
    IVCNarrow → fan to the widest diameter; confirm it enters the RA.
    EverywherePosition the patient first (left lateral decubitus for parasternal and apical windows). Start deep, then come up. Breathe out.

    09 · LV systolic function

    Eyeball first. Then EPSS, shortening and thickening.

    GradeWhat you see (grade in at least two views)
    HyperdynamicSmall cavity, near-obliterated at end-systole, kissing papillary muscles
    NormalBrisk symmetric inward motion and thickening; anterior mitral leaflet nearly touches the septum
    ReducedVisibly less inward motion; mitral leaflet stays well away from the septum
    Severely reducedDilated, barely moving LV; mitral leaflet opens weakly; smoke may appear

    Normal LVEF is about 52–72% in men and 54–74% in women (ASE/EACVI 2015). An experienced eye grades well, but agreement with formal measurement is only moderate for learners. Back up your eyeball with:

    E-point septal separation (EPSS) · PLAX M-mode through the anterior leaflet tip
    • The shortest distance between the leaflet's early-diastolic E-point and the septum. Normal is under about 7 mm.
    • EPSS >7 mm had 100% sensitivity but only about 52% specificity for LVEF ≤30% in ED patients (McKaigney 2014). A small EPSS is reassuring.
    • Falsely large in mitral stenosis, aortic regurgitation (the jet pushes the leaflet back) and asymmetric septal hypertrophy. Unreliable with an off-axis cut or a regional abnormality that spares the base.
    Fractional shortening (FS) · PLAX M-mode just beyond the leaflet tips
    FS = (LVIDd − LVIDs) / LVIDd × 100
    • Normal is roughly 25–45%. FS is one dimension: it ignores the apex and misleads with a regional wall motion abnormality.
    • Normal LVIDd is about 42–58 mm in men and 38–52 mm in women (ASE 2015). Above this means LV dilatation.
    Wall thickening
    • Judge thickening, not just inward motion. A scarred, akinetic segment can be pulled inwards by its neighbours (tethering), but it won't thicken.
    • Regional thinning (<6 mm) with akinesis suggests old infarction.
    • A new regional abnormality in a shocked patient means ACS until proven otherwise. Get a formal echo and call cardiology.
    Remember: a vasodilated, septic LV can look hyperdynamic despite intrinsic dysfunction. Re-look once vasopressors restore afterload.

    10 · RV size and function

    Is the RV big? Is the septum flat? Is it moving?

    Size · A4C at end-diastole, basal diameter compared with the LV
    • Normal RV is less than about two-thirds of the LV. RV:LV ratio <0.6 is normal; 0.6–1.0 is dilated; ≥1.0 is severely dilated.
    • ESC 2019 PE guideline: an RV/LV diameter ratio ≥1.0 is a marker of RV dysfunction. The 2026 AHA/ACC PE guideline also asks for RV size, RV/LV ratio, TAPSE and TR velocity, and, in patients without hypotension or shock, prefers echo to CT for short-term risk stratification. Echo signs of RV strain do not diagnose PE: confirm it with dedicated PE imaging.
    • RV basal diameter >41 mm is dilated (ASE 2015).
    • Check the RV apex: a normal LV forms the cardiac apex. An RV that reaches the apex is dilated.
    Shape · PSAX septal position
    • D-sign throughout the cycle → pressure overload (PE, pulmonary hypertension, ARDS/high PEEP).
    • D-sign in diastole only → volume overload (e.g. ASD, severe TR).
    • Paradoxical septal motion towards the LV.
    Function
    • TAPSE: M-mode at the lateral tricuspid annulus in A4C; longitudinal excursion of the annulus. <17 mm is abnormal (Lang 2015; ASE right heart guideline 2025). The older Rudski 2010 guideline and ESC 2019 PE use <16 mm. Angle- and load-dependent. Normal is about 20–25 mm.
    • McConnell sign: akinesis of the mid RV free wall with preserved apical motion. Original report in acute PE: sensitivity 77%, specificity 94% (McConnell 1996). Also seen in RV infarction, so it is not pathognomonic.
    FeatureAcute (e.g. high-risk PE)Chronic (pulmonary hypertension)
    RV free wallThin (≤5 mm)Hypertrophied (>5 mm)
    TR peak velocityUsually modest (a thin RV can't generate high pressure; PASP rarely >60 mmHg)Often high
    RAMildly dilatedLarge
    ClinicalSuddenLong history, often known

    A thin-walled RV can't generate a pulmonary artery systolic pressure much above 60 mmHg. A very high TR gradient therefore points to chronic disease, or acute-on-chronic disease. Estimate PASP ≈ 4 × (peak TR velocity)² + RAP (from the IVC), e.g. TR 3.0 m/s → 36 + 8 = 44 mmHg (ASE right heart guideline 2025).

    12 · Effusion and tamponade

    Pericardial or pleural? The descending aorta decides.

    Pericardial effusion · an anechoic space between epicardium and parietal pericardium
    • In PLAX, pericardial fluid lies anterior to the descending aorta and usually tapers at the posterior AV groove; it rarely tracks far behind the LA (oblique sinus).
    • Pleural effusion lies posterior to the descending aorta, extends beyond the heart, and may contain a 'jellyfish' of collapsed lung.
    • Epicardial fat: usually anterior only, grainy or speckled (not purely black), moves with the heart, and does not appear posteriorly. An isolated anterior echo-free space is fat until proven otherwise.
    • Size (end-diastole, ESC 2015): small <10 mm, moderate 10–20 mm, large >20 mm.
    • Circumferential or loculated? Loculated effusions are common after cardiac surgery and can compress only one chamber.
    Tamponade physiology · the low-pressure chambers collapse first
    • RA systolic collapse (inversion at end-diastole to early systole): the earliest and most sensitive sign. Collapse lasting more than one-third of the cardiac cycle is more specific.
    • RV diastolic collapse (free wall pushed in during early diastole): less sensitive but more specific.
    • Plethoric IVC (>2.1 cm, <50% respiratory change): very sensitive (present in >90% of tamponade), poorly specific. A collapsing IVC makes tamponade very unlikely, except when hypovolaemic.
    • Swinging heart (electrical alternans on ECG) with large effusions.
    • Advanced: exaggerated respiratory variation in mitral (>25%) and tricuspid (>40%) inflow on pulsed-wave Doppler.
    Tamponade is a clinical diagnosis (hypotension, tachycardia, raised JVP, pulsus paradoxus) that ultrasound supports and speeds up. Small but rapid effusions (trauma, post-procedure) can tamponade. Large chronic ones may not.
    Pitfalls and management
    • Pulmonary hypertension or RV hypertrophy: the RV may not collapse.
    • Hypovolaemia: 'low-pressure tamponade' with a non-plethoric IVC.
    • Positive-pressure ventilation alters respiratory variation.
    • Use the ECG or M-mode to time collapse to systole or diastole.
    • If tamponade is likely, prepare for drainage (echo-guided pericardiocentesis or surgery). Avoid induction and positive-pressure ventilation if at all possible. Give a modest fluid bolus as a bridge only.

    13 · IVC and fluid responsiveness

    The IVC tells you about RA pressure. Whether stroke volume will rise is a different question.

    Spontaneously breathing: estimating RA pressure (ASE 2025, unchanged from Rudski 2010)
    IVC diameterCollapse with sniffEstimated RAP
    ≤2.1 cm>50%3 mmHg (0–5)
    ≤2.1 cm<50%8 mmHg (5–10)
    >2.1 cm>50%8 mmHg (5–10)
    >2.1 cm<50%15 mmHg (10–20)
    Collapsibility index = (Dmax − Dmin) / Dmax
    Fluid responsiveness: stroke volume rises by about 10–15% after a fluid challenge
    • Mechanically ventilated, fully passive: the IVC distends with each positive-pressure breath. Distensibility index (Dmax − Dmin)/Dmin >18% predicted response (Barbier 2004). ΔIVC (Dmax − Dmin)/mean >12% predicted response (Feissel 2004). Overall accuracy of IVC variation is only moderate (meta-analysis in ventilated shocked patients: pooled sensitivity 69%, specificity 80%, AUROC 0.82; Huang 2018); use it as one input, not a test.
    • Valid only with no spontaneous breathing effort, tidal volume about ≥8 mL/kg and no raised intra-abdominal pressure; a regular rhythm helps.
    • Spontaneously breathing: much weaker. A large collapse (e.g. ≥40–50%) makes response likely, but a small collapse does not rule it out (high specificity, low sensitivity; Airapetian 2015). Breathing effort varies breath to breath.
    The useful extremes
    • A small (<1 cm), nearly fully collapsing IVC: fluid is likely to be tolerated, and a response is more likely. Confirm with PLR/VTI where possible; forceful inspiration and off-centre cuts can fake collapse.
    • A plethoric (>2.1–2.5 cm), fixed IVC: fluid is unlikely to help. Look for the reason (RV failure, tamponade, tension pneumothorax, severe TR, fluid overload).
    • The middle ground is uninformative. Use a passive leg raise with VTI (next slide).
    Caveats

    Raised intra-abdominal pressure compresses the IVC. RV failure, TR or tamponade distend it whatever the volume status. High PEEP dilates it. Forceful breathing exaggerates collapse. The cylinder effect and translational movement fake collapse. Athletes can have a large IVC. Being fluid-responsive is not a reason to give fluid. It must also be needed and safe.

    14 · Brief advanced: LVOT VTI and PLR

    Measure stroke volume, give a fluid challenge with no fluid, measure again.

    LVOT VTI (velocity–time integral)
    • Take an apical 5-chamber view (tilt anteriorly from A4C). Place the pulsed-wave Doppler gate in the LVOT about 0.5 cm below the aortic valve, aligned with flow. Trace the envelope.
    • VTI is the 'stroke distance' (cm). Stroke volume = VTI × LVOT area (π × (LVOT diameter/2)²).
    • A typical normal value is about 18–22 cm. Values well below about 10 cm signal a very low stroke volume.
    • Use trends in VTI. Keep the same gate position and angle for repeat measurements.
    Passive leg raise (PLR)
    • Start semi-recumbent at 45°. Lower the trunk flat and raise the legs to 45° in one movement. This returns about 300 mL of venous blood, reversibly.
    • Measure VTI (or cardiac output) at baseline and at 30–90 s.
    • A rise of ≥10% predicts fluid responsiveness. Pooled sensitivity 85%, specificity 91%, AUROC 0.95 for cardiac output changes (Monnet 2016 meta-analysis). In spontaneously breathing patients, a PLR-induced rise in stroke volume (from LVOT VTI) ≥12.5% predicted response: sensitivity 77%, specificity 100% (Lamia 2007).
    • Measure flow, not blood pressure: pulse pressure changes after PLR are much less sensitive.
    • Pitfalls: pain, coughing or waking increase sympathetic tone and give a false positive. Raised intra-abdominal pressure gives a false negative. Not suitable with raised ICP or with unstable spine or leg fractures. Start from semi-recumbent: starting supine recruits less volume.
    If the VTI rises with PLR, and fluid is needed and safe, give a bolus and remeasure.

    15 · Fluid tolerance and VExUS

    Will the patient respond to fluid? Will they tolerate it? These are separate questions.

    Responsiveness vs tolerance
    • Fluid responsiveness: stroke volume rises with a fluid challenge (previous two slides). It tells you fluid could help.
    • Fluid tolerance: the patient can take more fluid without harm from venous congestion or oedema (Kattan 2022). A patient can be both responsive and intolerant, for example a septic patient with ARDS or capillary leak, or with intra-abdominal hypertension, whose stroke volume still rises with fluid. A congested, failing RV is usually neither responsive nor tolerant.
    • High right atrial pressure is transmitted back to the liver, gut and kidneys. Venous congestion lowers the organ perfusion gradient, and the kidney is especially vulnerable.
    • VExUS (venous excess ultrasound; Beaubien-Souligny 2020) grades how far that pressure reaches the organs, using the IVC plus three venous Doppler waveforms.
    The three Doppler patterns (time each one against the ECG)
    VeinNormalMildSevere
    Hepatic veinS > DS < D (S still towards the heart)S reversed
    Portal vein pulsatility<30%30–49%≥50%
    Intrarenal veinContinuousDiscontinuous, biphasic (S and D)Discontinuous, monophasic (D only)
    Portal pulsatility fraction = (Vmax − Vmin) / Vmax × 100

    Grade 0IVC <2 cm: no significant congestion (Doppler not needed for the grade)
    Grade 1IVC ≥2 cm with normal and/or mildly abnormal patterns only: mild

    16 · VExUS in practice

    Four looks, one ECG, and a lot of caveats.

    Acquisition: phased-array or curvilinear probe, ECG trace on, breath held if possible
    LookHow
    IVCSubcostal long axis, about 1–2 cm from the cavo-atrial junction (slide 07). Use the maximum diameter. In ventilated patients on PEEP the IVC is often ≥2 cm: rely on the Doppler patterns and state that the IVC step is confounded.
    Hepatic veinSubcostal or right intercostal view of a large hepatic vein (often the middle) near the IVC. Colour, then a PW gate in the vein. Name each wave against the ECG: S follows the QRS, D follows the T wave.
    Portal veinRight mid-axillary intercostal view, coronal plane: the portal vein runs through the liver with bright walls. PW gate in the main or right portal vein; measure Vmax and Vmin over one cycle.
    Intrarenal veinRight kidney, posterolateral approach. Colour Doppler with a low velocity scale to find interlobar vessels. A wide PW gate captures the artery above and the vein below the baseline. Use a slow sweep.
    Caveats: when the waveforms mislead
    • Atrial fibrillation: no A wave and a small S wave, so S < D is common at baseline. Interpret the hepatic vein with care.
    • Severe tricuspid regurgitation: the regurgitant jet itself reverses the hepatic S wave. The finding reflects the TR more than overall venous pressure, so grade on the portal and renal veins and the IVC too.
    • Cirrhosis or portal hypertension: a stiff liver flattens the hepatic vein waveform (often monophasic) and can damp or even reverse portal flow, whatever the RAP. Both hepatic and portal signals are unreliable; lean on the IVC, the renal vein and the echo.
    • Fit, thin, young people: portal pulsatility can be a normal variant. A pulsatile portal vein with a small IVC is grade 0.
    • CKD and renal parenchymal disease: intrarenal venous patterns are less reliable. This is also the hardest window; check you are not sampling the main renal vein.
    • Raised intra-abdominal pressure compresses the IVC and can alter the waveforms. High PEEP and chronic pulmonary hypertension raise RAP without volume excess.
    • VExUS shows pressure, not volume. It cannot tell RV failure from fluid overload: the echo does that.
    VExUS grading, step by step (schematic)
    What it does and does not predict
    • Derivation (145 patients after cardiac surgery): grade 3 predicted AKI, HR 3.69 (95% CI 1.65–8.24), 2.82 after adjustment. At ICU admission grade 3 was highly specific (96%) but insensitive (about 27%) (Beaubien-Souligny 2020).
    • General ICU (145 patients, multicentre): VExUS ≥2 in about one in five at admission and not associated with AKI or 28-day mortality (Andrei 2023). In sepsis (108 patients, four ICUs), VExUS ≥2 was not independently associated with AKI: adjusted OR 1.82 (0.62–5.31) (Song 2025).
    • Meta-analysis (9 observational studies, 1036 patients): VExUS ≥2 associated with AKI, OR 2.63 (1.06–6.54) with high heterogeneity; strongest after cardiac surgery (OR 3.86). No association with mortality (Melo 2025).
    • Physiology: grade correlated with right atrial pressure at catheterisation, better than IVC size alone (pilot study; Longino 2023).
    • Guiding treatment: in cardiorenal syndrome type 1 (RCT, 140 patients), VExUS-guided diuresis achieved decongestion more often, but kidney function recovery and 90-day survival did not differ (Islas-Rodríguez 2024). No trial yet shows better hard outcomes.
    Grade 2–3 (expert consensus, not trial evidence): stop routine fluid boluses and find the cause on the echo (RV failure, PE, tamponade, tension pneumothorax, severe TR, high PEEP, fluid overload). Treat obstruction directly: drain tamponade, decompress a pneumothorax, reperfuse PE. Consider decongestion (diuretic or ultrafiltration) only when the cause is volume overload or RV/cardiorenal congestion, with perfusion pressure supported, then rescan: the waveforms improve as RAP falls. Grade 0 does not mean fluid will help: that is the PLR question.
    17 · Red flags and limits

    See the red flag, then hand it to an echocardiographer.

    Gross valve and structural red flags: recognise and refer, don't grade
    • Heavily calcified, immobile aortic valve with restricted opening, ± LV hypertrophy: suspect severe aortic stenosis. This matters before a spinal or induction (keep SVR and sinus rhythm; avoid tachycardia).
    • Flail mitral leaflet / ruptured papillary muscle: leaflet tip prolapses into the LA, with a new murmur after MI. Also look for a new VSD (septal defect with flow on colour Doppler).
    • Dynamic LVOT obstruction with SAM: the anterior mitral leaflet moves towards the septum in systole, in a small, hyperdynamic, hypertrophied LV. Inotropes, tachycardia and hypovolaemia make it worse.
    • Large, mobile mass: vegetation, LV apical thrombus, thrombus in transit in the RA/RV.
    • Dilated aortic root (>about 4 cm in PLAX) or an intimal flap with pericardial effusion: suspect type A dissection.
    • Colour Doppler: a big, wide regurgitant jet filling much of an atrium is a red flag, not a grade.
    • Prosthetic valve that looks rocking or immobile.
    Echo in cardiac arrest
    • Get the probe on (subcostal) during compressions. Record a clip at the pulse check and review it after CPR restarts; keep each pause under 10 s, with someone counting aloud.
    • Look for organised wall motion (pseudo-PEA: treat as profound shock), standstill, a large effusion, or an empty heart. A dilated RV is common after any arrest, so it does not prove PE on its own.
    • Ultrasound must not lengthen pauses in compressions; if it does, stop scanning.
    FoCUS pitfalls
    • An off-axis or foreshortened view makes structures look the wrong size or shape.
    • Grading the LV on one view.
    • Calling a D-sign on an oval PSAX.
    • Fat called as effusion; pleural fluid called as pericardial.
    • Aorta called as IVC.
    • Anchoring: the scan looked 'normal', so the shock must be distributive.
    • Vasopressors and inotropes change what you see, so rescan after a change.
    When to call for a formal echo (TTE/TOE)
    • Image quality is inadequate to answer the question.
    • An unexpected finding: valve red flag, mass, regional wall motion abnormality, dilated aortic root.
    • The finding doesn't fit the clinical picture, or the patient is not improving as expected.
    • Quantification is needed (LVEF, valve gradients, PASP, diastolic function).
    • Suspected endocarditis, dissection, mechanical complications of MI, or prosthetic valve dysfunction. These usually need TOE.

    Document: views obtained, image quality, findings as answers to the five questions, and your limitations.

    Take home
    1. Ask a question you can answer. Get two views. Answer yes / no / can't tell.
    2. Start deep, use the cardiac preset, and keep the marker convention consistent.
    3. Eyeball the LV in two views. Back it up with EPSS and fractional shortening, and judge thickening rather than motion.
