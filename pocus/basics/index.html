Foundations · 02

What decision could this scan change?

“Do a POCUS” is a task. “Large pericardial collection in this shocked patient?” is a question.

  • Before scanning: what you are looking for, what finding changes management, what you will do if images are inadequate.
  • A focused scan has a defined scope; it is not a complete echo.
  • Unstable patient: resuscitate and assess together. Don’t prolong scanning for a textbook view. [1]

Acquire → describe → integrate → reassess

Acquire: an interpretable view; confirm surprises in another plane.

Describe: what is visible, before naming a cause.

Integrate: with perfusion, ventilation, examination and prior information.

Reassess: did treatment work? Use comparable observations.

Three possible answers: present, absent (within the scan’s limits) or indeterminate. “Not visualised” is indeterminate.

Try it: rewrite “scan for fluid status”

“With impaired perfusion, does a valid dynamic test show increased forward flow, and are there signs more fluid would be poorly tolerated?” This separates responsiveness from the decision to give fluid.

Foundations · 03

The machine estimates depth from time.

The transducer sends a short pulse and listens. Travel time sets depth; echo strength sets brightness.

Depth = sound speed × round-trip time ÷ 2
Assumed soft-tissue speed ≈ 1540 m/s

Divide by two: sound goes there and back. B-mode builds a 2D image from many such lines; strong boundaries are bright, weak echoes dark. [2]

The machine assumes a direct path. Extra travel, refraction or an unexpected reflector puts an echo where no structure exists: the basis of artefacts.

Worked example

Reflector at 5 cm: round trip 10 cm, about 65 microseconds. A repeated echo arriving later is displayed deeper, though the reflector never moved.

Too fast to see; the lab slows the pulse and labels both paths.

Schematic simulation · round-trip timing

Move the reflector deeper; predict the echo time. Timing is calculated; the pulse is slowed.

Foundations · 04

Choose enough resolution—and enough reach.

Higher frequency: shorter wavelength, but more attenuation. Use the highest frequency that still shows the target.

Signal that never returns from depth cannot be recovered; more receiver gain mainly amplifies noise. Resolution also depends on pulse length, beam geometry and processing, not frequency alone. [3]

Schematic simulation · resolution versus attenuation

Compare 2, 5 and 12 MHz. Model: 0.5 dB/cm/MHz over the round trip; real tissue, probes and compensation differ. Grey level is not a clinical measurement.

Before adjusting gain, ask

Target too deep for this probe? Rib, air or poor contact in the way? Would another window shorten the path? Fix the acoustic route before amplifying.

Foundations · 05

Match the footprint to the window.

ProbeUseful starting pointTrade-off
Linear arraySuperficial vessels, needle guidance, pleural-line detail.High-frequency detail; broad footprint blocked by ribs; deep structures poorly seen.
Curvilinear arrayAbdomen, diaphragm and deeper pleural or lung-base findings.Wide deep field; curved near field, less superficial detail.
Phased arrayCardiac windows between ribs; restricted access.Small footprint, sector field; very superficial structures harder to resolve.

Frequency bands overlap and vary by machine. Choose the preset too: it changes processing, dynamic range and more. Confirm it before comparing serial images. [3]

Practical sequence

  1. Target and its approximate depth.
  2. Footprint that fits the window.
  3. Preset.
  4. Find the anatomy, then optimise depth, gain and focus.

One probe does not answer every question

A crisp pleural line on a linear probe does not show the full depth of a large collection. Change probe when the question changes.

Teaching: the learner justifies the probe before scanning.

Foundations · 06

Confirm the marker before interpreting left and right.

The probe marker matches the screen marker. Which side of the screen it sits on depends on the convention.

Don’t rely on a memorised “left is right” rule. Touch one end of the probe or make a small movement, find that screen edge, then assign anatomical directions. Cardiac and general conventions can differ.

Schematic simulation · physical slice and screen mapping

Reverse the probe, then move the screen marker right: why does either mirror the image? Rotate off short axis: an oblique cut elongates the lumen.

Movements: slide moves the footprint; rotate changes the plane; tilt/fan changes beam direction; rock centres the target in the plane. One small adjustment at a time.

Why two planes?

A circle in one plane could be a vessel, cyst or other fluid space. Follow continuity and check the orthogonal plane; one slice is an incomplete 3D description.

Foundations · 07

Optimise the image in a repeatable order.

  1. Find the target; include its far boundary; trim excess depth.
  2. Gain: borders visible, fluid not filled with noise.
  3. Time-gain compensation for depth-dependent differences.
  4. Focus at the target. [3]

Schematic simulation · the same anatomy, different settings

Try depth 3 then 8 cm; low then excessive gain; then move focus off 5 cm. Simplified phantom; brightness is not calibrated to a real scanner.

Gain is not acoustic output

Receiver gain amplifies returning signals; it is not transmit power. With poor penetration, more gain gives a brighter but less interpretable image.

Do not crop away the answer

Reduce depth only after finding the target’s boundaries. A neat near field that excludes a deep collection solves the wrong problem.

Foundations · 08

A sharper still image can be a poorer moving image.

Spatial resolution

Axial: along the beam; shorter pulses help. Lateral: across the beam; a narrower beam helps. Elevational: the slice has thickness; echoes can enter from outside the plane.

Close structures can merge into one apparent edge, which may not be the true edge.

Temporal resolution

Each line takes time to send and receive, so more depth, a wider sector and extra focal zones lower frame rate. A narrow sector on a located cardiac target makes motion easier to assess. [3]

Don’t narrow so far that you lose the chamber relationships that identify the view. Useful first, then fast.

Exercise: image a moving valve with a broad deep field, then a narrow shallow one. Compare motion continuity and what anatomy was lost.

Why magnification is not necessarily more information

Zooming a frozen image enlarges pixels; it recovers no detail. Optimise during acquisition: a poor image stays a poor basis for measurement.

Foundations · 09

A feature on the image may not be a structure.

An artefact is a clue to how sound travelled. Test it: change window, angle or settings; check another plane.

Schematic simulation · physical path versus displayed image

Teaching models, not a full catalogue. Real scans can mix mechanisms.

MechanismRecogniseCheck
ReverberationRepeated lines from extra sound travel.Regular spacing, strong proximal reflector; change angle or window.
ShadowingDark beyond a strongly attenuating or reflecting region.Find the proximal cause and scan around it; darkness is not fluid.
Posterior enhancementBrighter behind a low-attenuation path.Compare tissue at the same depth; check the overlying structure in two planes.

Side lobes, mirror images and slice thickness also create misleading echoes. If views disagree, suspect artefact before diagnosis. [4]

Foundations · 10

The beam measures the component of velocity along it.

Doppler shift = 2 × transmit frequency × velocity × cos θ ÷ sound speed

Here the scanner assumes 0°. At 60° it displays half the true speed; at 90° the shift is zero despite flow. The angle is beam-to-flow, not probe-to-skin. [3]

Schematic simulation · angle and measured velocity

Set 0°, 60° and 90°; predict the peak each time. No Doppler signal does not mean no flow.

At the bedside: align, keep the sample in the intended structure, get a clean envelope, then trace. A number does not repair a poor acquisition.

Cardiac Doppler: find a window aligned with flow rather than angle-correcting. Vascular studies have their own angle-correction protocols; don’t apply them to intracardiac measurements.

Foundations · 11

Different modes answer different questions.

ModeWhat it providesWhat it does not provide
B-mode2D anatomy and motion.A complete 3D survey from one slice.
M-modeMotion over time along one selected line.Correction of an oblique or misplaced line.
Colour DopplerA Doppler map over a chosen region.An artery/vein label. Red/blue show direction relative to the beam, per the displayed map.
Pulsed-wave DopplerVelocity at a chosen sample volume.Unlimited velocity; sampling causes aliasing.
Continuous-wave DopplerHigh velocities along the beam, without the PW Nyquist limit.The depth of the fastest signal.

LVOT flow example

PW in the LVOT samples local flow. CW may include faster aortic-valve flow. Both are systolic; they are not interchangeable.

Colour example

Colour locates flow and guides sampling. Poor alignment, wrong scale, excess wall filter or low gain can hide real flow; excess colour gain spreads outside the lumen.

Ask the learner

“Which mode answers your question, and what assumption does it add?” Answer in terms of sampling, direction, location or timing.

Foundations · 12

Sampling can make fast forward flow wrap below baseline.

PW samples repeatedly. A shift above half the pulse-repetition frequency (PRF) aliases.

Model: 2.5 MHz, 0°. Raise velocity or lower PRF. Flow stays forward even when the envelope wraps to the other side.

Schematic simulation · sampled velocity and wraparound

Narrow-band waveform to isolate aliasing. Real spectra hold a range of velocities, broadened by flow, sample volume and instrument effects.

Fix the acquisition

  • Raise scale/PRF if possible (deeper sampling limits PRF).
  • Lower the Doppler transmit frequency.
  • CW for high-velocity measurement.
  • Keep alignment; never angle away to hide high velocity.

What baseline shift changes

Reallocates the range above and below zero. Helps one-directional flow, but the total unambiguous range, set by PRF, is unchanged.

Foundations · 13

Quality comes from a repeatable sequence.

  1. Prepare: explain, identify the patient, position, probe and preset.
  2. Orient: screen marker, landmarks, expected relationships.
  3. Optimise: one control or movement at a time; recognise off-axis views.
  4. Observe: several cardiac or respiratory cycles, not one good frame.
  5. Confirm: unexpected findings in another view or plane.
  6. Save: representative clips, labels and measurements in the approved system.

A useful stopping rule

Non-diagnostic image? Stop trying to “make it normal”. State the limitation, change window or get help. An inadequate negative scan cannot close the question.

Keep serial scans comparable

Record position, ventilation, rhythm, blood pressure and treatment changes. Use the same acquisition and measurement method. If conditions changed, a difference may not be a treatment effect.

Store clips so others can review them. Competence needs observed acquisition, interpretation and integration, not just a scan count. [1]

Useful feedback: one specific correction (“Too deep: reduce depth, keep the far boundary”), then let the learner repeat.

Foundations · 14

Prepare the equipment as deliberately as the image.

Before and after the patient

  • Hand hygiene; remove gel and visible soiling; disinfect as the transducer and examination require. Follow the manufacturer (compatibility, contact time) and local policy.
  • Intact skin: low-level disinfection between patients. Internal and intraoperative probes: higher standard. A cover does not replace cleaning and disinfection.
  • Percutaneous procedures: single-use cover matched to sterility needs; sterile single-use gel where required. Clean cable, controls and touched surfaces. [5]

Use ultrasound prudently

No ionising radiation, but not biologically inert. ALARA: only the output and time the question needs.

Know where thermal and mechanical indices are shown. Don’t raise output instead of fixing window, receiver settings or probe. Training scans also need consent, infection control and proportionate exposure. [6]

Before a sterile procedure: decide which hand touches the patient, which the controls, and how probe and cable stay in the sterile field.

Foundations · 15

A clear report makes the scan usable.

Document indication, conditions, scope, image quality, findings, interpretation and action; units and method for measurements; operator, date, time and where images are stored. Communicate urgent findings directly; a saved clip may not be found. [7]

Constructed example: a limited scan

Question: gross ventricular dysfunction in postoperative hypotension.

Conditions: supine, mechanically ventilated, sinus rhythm, vasoactive support ongoing.

Quality and findings: subcostal: LV motion visible, endocardial borders incomplete. Apical and parasternal non-diagnostic.

Interpretation: cannot reliably assess global or regional LV function.

Action: continue shock assessment; urgent expert echo. Representative clip saved.

Separate description from inference

  • “Dark space beside the heart”: description; needs localisation.
  • “Pericardial effusion”: needs anatomical confirmation.
  • “Tamponade”: adds a haemodynamic interpretation.

Don’t write “normal echo” for a focused scan. List what was not assessed and what remains unresolved.

Identifiable images stay in approved systems; teaching copies need authorisation and de-identification.

Foundations · 16

The near field is crisp. The question is still unanswered.

Dyspnoea. A high-frequency linear probe shows a clear pleural line, but not the deep extent of a suspected basal collection.

The learner turns gain up until the image is uniformly bright; the deep boundary stays indistinct. They report “small effusion” because only a small dark area is visible.

What should you change before accepting that conclusion?

Reveal the reasoning

Change the acquisition: a lower-frequency curvilinear or phased-array probe, the right preset, and a window including diaphragm, adjacent organ and the whole collection. Recheck depth and gain once the anatomy is found.

The view limited the apparent size; gain cannot restore a missing deep boundary. Still undefined? Document the limitation; get further imaging or expert assessment.

Frequency lab: which control improves reach, and what superficial detail is lost?

Transfer: the best probe answers the current question. A good near field is not an adequate deep survey.

Foundations · 17

A bright line appears twice.

Focused cardiac scan: a strong interface, then a fainter parallel line at a regular interval, apparently inside a chamber.

The learner calls it a membrane. In a second window the line shifts relative to the anatomy and fades.

How would you distinguish a real structure from an artefact?

Reveal the reasoning

Describe it until tested. Does it follow anatomical boundaries, move coherently and reproduce in another plane? A strong proximal reflector with regular spacing suggests reverberation.

Change probe position and beam angle; optimise gain without erasing real boundaries. Artefacts change with these manoeuvres, but disappearance alone does not prove nothing is there. Important uncertainty → expert imaging.

Artefact lab: repeated travel creates a second line without a second reflector.

Transfer: confirm surprises in independent views; no major diagnosis from one ambiguous line.

Foundations · 18

The trace crosses baseline after you lower the scale.

A PW waveform was entirely above baseline. After lowering the velocity scale, its peak wraps below. Sample volume and haemodynamics are unchanged.

Has the blood reversed direction?

Reveal the reasoning

Timing and the change with scale mean aliasing. Raise PRF/scale and view the whole envelope. Check baseline, sample site, alignment and whether PW is the right mode.

True reverse flow does occur elsewhere; identify it by a coherent, well-acquired waveform and anatomy, not colour or sign alone.

Lab: fix true flow at 1.2 m/s, lower PRF from 10 to 2 kHz. Nothing changed in the patient; the sampling limit did.

Transfer: a setting can change the pattern without changing the patient. Exclude an acquisition cause before a physiological one.
