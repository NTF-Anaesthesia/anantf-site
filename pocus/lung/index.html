POCUS / Interactive tutorial

Lung ultrasound.

Air hides the lung. The artefacts tell you what it is doing.

The lung is air. We read the artefacts it makes.

For adult anaesthesia and ICU trainees: residents, MOs and ICU registrars. By the end you will be able to:

  • Explain why a normal lung shows A-lines, and why fluid in the interstitium turns them into B-lines.
  • Find the BLUE points and scan 8 or 12 regions in a fixed order.
  • Rule in or rule out pneumothorax with four signs: sliding, B-lines, lung pulse and lung point.
  • Tell interstitial syndrome, consolidation and effusion apart, and estimate an effusion's volume.
  • Run the BLUE protocol for acute respiratory failure, and know what FALLS adds in shock.
  • Calculate an LUS aeration score and know where it helps (PEEP, weaning).
  • Use lung ultrasound in theatre and recovery: endobronchial intubation, atelectasis, and pneumothorax after a line or block.

Why it matters

In a systematic review, lung ultrasound detected pneumothorax with a pooled sensitivity of about 91% (specificity 98%), against about 50% for supine chest radiography (Alrajhi 2012). In the original BLUE study, lung and venous ultrasound reached the correct diagnosis in 90.5% of patients with acute respiratory failure (Lichtenstein 2008).

01 · The I-AIM framework for lung

Four questions, in this order, every time.

I-AIM is the structure we use for gastric ultrasound (Perlas et al., BJA 2016), applied here to the lung. Tap a card to open its checklist.

What is the clinical question, and would the answer change what I do? Examples:
  • dyspnoea or hypoxia
  • shock
  • suspected pneumothorax after a procedure
  • checking tube position
  • PEEP or weaning decisions
  • an effusion before drainage
Choose the probe and preset, mark the points (BLUE points, or 8 or 12 regions), scan both sides in the same order, and record what you see at each point.
  1. Is there a pleural line, and is it sliding?
  2. A-lines or B-lines? If B-lines, how many, and are they coalescent?
  3. Is there consolidation (tissue-like lung)?
  4. Is there an effusion?
  5. If sliding is absent: is there a lung pulse, any B-lines, a lung point?
Then combine the points into a profile.
Fit the profile into a protocol (BLUE for respiratory failure, FALLS or RUSH for shock) and into the clinical picture. Lung ultrasound adds to clinical judgement; it does not replace it.
Rule: a scan you cannot interpret is inconclusive, not negative.

02 · Artefact physics

The bat sign, A-lines and B-lines.

Bat sign

Scan longitudinally across two ribs. The rib shadows are the bat's wings and the pleural line, about 0.5 cm below the rib line in adults, is its body. Always find the bat sign first, so you know you are looking at the pleura and not at a rib or a fascial plane.

Pleural line and A-lines

The pleural line is the bright line where parietal and visceral pleura meet. Air in the lung beyond it reflects almost all of the beam.

A-lines are horizontal, bright, evenly spaced reverberation artefacts: the beam bounces between the pleura and the probe face, so each A-line sits at a whole multiple of the skin-to-pleura distance. A-lines mean air lies below the pleura: normal aerated lung, or free air in a pneumothorax. A-lines by themselves cannot tell the two apart; sliding can.

B-lines (Volpicelli 2012 consensus)

Discrete, laser-like, vertical, hyperechoic reverberation artefacts that:

  • arise from the pleural line,
  • extend to the bottom of the screen without fading,
  • move in time with lung sliding.

Lichtenstein also notes that they erase the A-lines.

They are produced where thickened, fluid-filled interlobular septa or partly deflated alveoli sit next to air. One or two B-lines in a space, especially at the lateral bases, can be normal. A space with 3 or more B-lines in a longitudinal scan is positive.

Lookalikes

Z-lines (and short I-lines) are short, ill-defined and fade within a few centimetres; they do not erase A-lines and are not B-lines. Note: older papers, including Lichtenstein 1997 and 1999, use 'comet-tail artefact' to mean the B-line itself. E-lines come from subcutaneous emphysema and start above the pleural line. A pleural line that cannot be seen means you cannot interpret B-lines.

03 · Probe, preset and points

Two hands on the chest: the BLUE points.

Probe
  • High-frequency linear (about 5–13 MHz), depth 4–6 cm: the best view of the pleural line, sliding, lung point and small subpleural consolidations. The usual choice in theatre and after procedures.
  • Curvilinear or microconvex (about 2–6 MHz), depth 12–18 cm: B-lines across the whole field, consolidation, effusion and the PLAPS point. A good all-round lung probe; Lichtenstein uses a microconvex for lung, heart and veins.
  • Phased array (cardiac): usable when it is the only probe on the machine, but its narrow near field shows the pleura poorly.
Settings and orientation

Choose a lung preset if the machine has one. Otherwise turn off tissue harmonics, compound imaging and heavy smoothing or persistence filters, because they suppress or blur artefacts. Set the focus at the pleural line.

Longitudinal (probe marker towards the head) across two ribs to find the bat sign. Then, if needed, rotate along the intercostal space to see more pleura.

BLUE points (Lichtenstein 2008)

Place both hands on the chest, thumbs excluded. The upper hand's little finger touches the lower edge of the clavicle, and the fingertips reach the midline.

  • Upper BLUE point: between the bases of the middle and ring fingers of the upper hand.
  • Lower BLUE point: middle of the palm of the lower hand.
  • Phrenic line: the lower edge of the lower hand, roughly where the diaphragm sits.
  • PLAPS point (posterolateral alveolar and/or pleural syndrome): where a horizontal line from the lower BLUE point meets the posterior axillary line. Scan as far posteriorly as the bed allows.
Regional schemes and gravity
  • 8 regions (Volpicelli 2012): anterior and lateral, upper and lower, on each side. Used to call interstitial syndrome.
  • 12 regions: adds posterior upper and lower on each side, bounded by the parasternal, anterior axillary and posterior axillary lines. Used for the LUS score.

Air rises and fluid falls. Look for pneumothorax at the anterior points (non-dependent in a supine patient) and for effusion and consolidation posteriorly at the bases.

04 · Normal lung

Sliding, A-lines and the seashore.

Normal aerated lung (A-pattern at that point)
  • Lung sliding: a shimmer, like ants marching along the pleural line, as the visceral pleura moves against the parietal pleura with each breath.
  • A-lines, with 2 or fewer B-lines.
M-mode

Seashore sign. Put the M-mode cursor through the pleural line between two ribs. Above the pleura, the static chest wall makes flat horizontal lines (the waves). Below it, the moving lung makes a granular, sandy pattern (the beach).

Barcode (stratosphere) sign. Horizontal lines above and below the pleural line, so no sandy texture. It means there is no movement at the pleural line. This fits pneumothorax but is not proof of it.

Lung pulse

When the lung is not ventilated but still lies against the chest wall, the heartbeat moves the pleural line in time with the ECG. A lung pulse shows the two pleural layers are touching, so there is no pneumothorax under the probe. Typical settings: apnoea, endobronchial intubation, complete atelectasis (Lichtenstein 2003).

Sliding has three meanings
  • Present: no pneumothorax at that point.
  • Absent, with a lung pulse or B-lines: no pneumothorax at that point (the lung touches the wall but is not moving).
  • Absent, with neither: pneumothorax is possible; look for a lung point.

Part II · Pathology

Air, water, tissue, fluid

The four core patterns: pneumothorax (free air), interstitial syndrome (water in the septa), consolidation (lung that looks like liver) and pleural effusion (anechoic fluid).

05 · Pneumothorax

Excluding it is easy. Proving it takes a lung point.

Signs that exclude pneumothorax at the point scanned (any one is enough)
  • Lung sliding. In Lichtenstein's ICU series (1995), preserved sliding effectively ruled out pneumothorax (NPV close to 100%). Absent sliding was sensitive but not specific (about 91% in that cohort, and lower in general ICU and ARDS patients).
  • B-lines. They arise from the visceral pleura, so they cannot be present where free air separates the two layers (Lichtenstein 1999).
  • Lung pulse.
  • Consolidation reaching the pleura, or an effusion with lung visible floating in it (sinusoid sign), at that point.

Beware hydropneumothorax: air sits anteriorly above the fluid level (look for a 'hydro-point'). In trauma, see eFAST for haemothorax and traumatic pneumothorax.

Signs that suggest pneumothorax

Absent sliding, A-lines only (the A'-profile anteriorly), barcode on M-mode. Sensitive, but not specific: see the pitfalls slide.

The sign that confirms it: the lung point

At the edge of the pneumothorax, the lung intermittently touches the chest wall during inspiration. In one view you see sliding (seashore) alternating with no sliding (barcode) at the same spot. In M-mode this is a seashore-barcode alternation in time with breathing. Specificity is 100%; sensitivity is about 66%, because a completely collapsed lung has no lung point (Lichtenstein 2000).

Size. The further lateral or posterior the lung point, the larger the pneumothorax. An anterior lung point suggests a small one; no lung point with complete collapse suggests a large one. This is a qualitative guide only.

Accuracy: pooled sensitivity about 91% and specificity about 98% for ultrasound, against about 50% sensitivity and 99% specificity for supine CXR (Alrajhi 2012, meta-analysis of mostly trauma studies).

06 · Simulation

Find the lung point.

Slide the probe from the sternum towards the axilla over a simulated anterior pneumothorax. Notice:

  • Over the pneumothorax: no sliding, A-lines, barcode.
  • At its edge: lung appears and disappears with each breath. The B-mode shows sliding coming in from one side; the M-mode alternates seashore and barcode.
  • Beyond the edge: normal sliding and seashore.

Then make the pneumothorax larger and watch the lung point move laterally, until there is no lung point at all (complete collapse).

Unstable with clinical tension? Decompress. Do not wait for a lung point.

07 · Interstitial syndrome

Three B-lines in a space. Two positive regions on each side.

Definitions (Volpicelli 2012)
  • Positive region: ≥3 B-lines in a longitudinal scan between two ribs.
  • Diffuse interstitial syndrome: ≥2 positive regions on each side, using the 8-region scan.
  • Severity: more B-lines close together, through to coalescent B-lines (white lung), means more loss of aeration.
Focal B-lines (one area)

Pneumonia or pneumonitis, atelectasis, contusion, pulmonary infarction, pleural disease, neoplasia.

Diffuse B-lines (both sides)
  • Cardiogenic pulmonary oedema
  • ARDS
  • Interstitial pneumonia or pneumonitis, including viral
  • Diffuse parenchymal lung disease, such as pulmonary fibrosis
B-profile in BLUE

Anterior lung rockets on both sides with lung sliding. For haemodynamic (cardiogenic) pulmonary oedema it had a sensitivity of 97% and a specificity of 95% (Lichtenstein 2008).

B-lines respond quickly to diuresis or dialysis and appear early when lung water rises. That is why they are useful for monitoring, and why FALLS uses them as a sign to stop giving fluid.

08 · Cardiogenic oedema or ARDS?

Smooth and uniform, or patchy and broken.

Both give bilateral B-lines. Features that favour ARDS or an inflammatory cause (Copetti 2008; Volpicelli 2012):

FeatureCardiogenic oedemaARDS / inflammatory
DistributionHomogeneous, bilateral, gravity-dependent gradientNon-homogeneous, with spared areas of normal lung next to abnormal
Pleural lineThin, smoothThickened, irregular, fragmented
Lung slidingPreservedReduced or absent in places
Subpleural consolidationsUnusualCommon, including anterior ones
EffusionsOften bilateralVariable, often small
Complete the picture with focused cardiac ultrasound

See focused cardiac ultrasound: a dilated, poorly contracting LV, a dilated IVC with little respiratory variation, or significant valve disease support a cardiogenic cause. A normal LV with B-lines should make you think of ARDS, pneumonitis or fibrosis.

Caveats: the two coexist, for example fluid overload in ARDS. Chronic fibrosis gives diffuse B-lines with an abnormal pleura and can mimic ARDS, so check the history and old imaging.

09 · Consolidation and atelectasis

When the lung looks like liver.

Consolidation

Once the alveoli fill with fluid, pus, blood or cells, or collapse, ultrasound passes into the lung.

  • Tissue-like sign (hepatisation). Lung with the echotexture of liver.
  • Shred (fractal) sign. The deep border, where consolidated lung meets aerated lung, is ragged and irregular. A lobar consolidation that reaches the whole lobe has no shred border and looks like a smooth, solid organ.
  • Ultrasound detected alveolar consolidation with about 90% sensitivity and 98% specificity against CT in ICU patients (Lichtenstein 2004).
Air bronchograms
  • Dynamic: the bright spots move outwards with inspiration, so the airway is patent and air is moving into it. Favours pneumonia over resorptive atelectasis (specificity 94%, PPV 97%; Lichtenstein 2009).
  • Static: trapped air that does not move. Seen in atelectasis and also in some pneumonias, so it does not separate them on its own.
  • Fluid bronchograms (anechoic tubes, no colour flow) suggest an obstructed airway, as in post-obstructive pneumonia.
Atelectasis (resorptive or compressive)
  • Static or absent air bronchograms
  • Signs of volume loss: raised hemidiaphragm, mediastinal shift, crowded parallel bronchograms
  • Lung pulse with absent sliding when the whole lung is collapsed (as after endobronchial intubation)
  • Compressive atelectasis floats in a large effusion (the 'jellyfish' or flapping lung).

Under general anaesthesia, dependent atelectasis appears within minutes of induction. It shows as posterior B-lines, then small subpleural and then larger consolidations.

10 · Pleural effusion

Anechoic above the diaphragm. Then measure it.

Where

Supine patient: posterior axillary line at the base (PLAPS point), probe marker towards the head, aiming slightly upwards. Find diaphragm, liver or spleen, and lung.

Signs
  • Anechoic (or echogenic) space between the parietal pleura and the lung, bounded below by the diaphragm.
  • Spine sign. Normally air above the diaphragm hides the vertebral bodies. With fluid, the bright line of the thoracic spine continues above the diaphragm.
  • Loss of the mirror image and of the curtain sign. Normally the aerated lung reflects the liver or spleen above the diaphragm and slides down like a curtain on inspiration; fluid abolishes both.
  • Quad sign (static): a space bordered by the pleural line, the two rib shadows and the lung line (visceral pleura) beneath.
  • Sinusoid sign (M-mode): the lung line moves towards the pleural line on inspiration and away on expiration, making a sine wave. It suggests free-flowing, low-viscosity fluid, which is usually aspirable. It does not tell you transudate from exudate.
  • Ultrasound shows small effusions that an erect CXR misses; an erect PA film usually needs about 200 mL or more to blunt the costophrenic angle (a lateral film about 50 mL); a supine film may miss much more.
What it looks like suggests what it is (Yang 1992)
  • Anechoic: either a transudate or an exudate.
  • Complex septated, complex non-septated or homogeneously echogenic: exudate (or blood or pus).
  • Septations or loculations: think empyema or haemothorax; may need an image-guided drain at a chosen spot.
Volume estimate: Balik formula (Balik 2006)

Ventilated patients, supine with the trunk raised about 15°. Measure the maximum separation between parietal and visceral pleura at the base, at end-expiration, in mm (Sep).

V (mL) ≈ 20 × Sep (mm) · e.g. Sep 30 mm ≈ 600 mL

It is an estimate: the mean error in the original study was about 160 mL. Different positions, tall patients and consolidated lung all affect it.

Drainage safety

In a series of 45 ultrasound-aided thoracenteses in ventilated patients there were no complications. Entry criteria were an interpleural gap of ≥15 mm, visible over 3 intercostal spaces (Lichtenstein 1999); this is an operator rule, not a validated cut-off. Prefer a lateral (mid-axillary) site, where intercostal arteries are less tortuous than posteriorly or medially, and use real-time guidance where you can. Mark the site in the patient's drainage position, aim just above a rib, and check for intercostal vessels with colour Doppler (see vascular access).

Part III · Protocols and scores

BLUE, FALLS and the LUS score

Turning single findings into decisions: the BLUE protocol for acute respiratory failure, FALLS for shock, and the LUS aeration score for PEEP and weaning.

11 · BLUE protocol

Respiratory failure in under three minutes.

The Bedside Lung Ultrasound in Emergency (BLUE) protocol (Lichtenstein & Mezière, Chest 2008) enrolled 301 adults admitted to ICU with acute respiratory failure; the 260 with a clear single diagnosis were analysed, and ultrasound matched the final diagnosis in 90.5%.

Profiles (anterior BLUE points, both sides)
  • A-profile: A-lines on both sides with sliding.
  • A'-profile: A-lines without sliding.
  • B-profile: anterior B-lines on both sides (lung rockets) with sliding.
  • B'-profile: B-lines on both sides without sliding.
  • A/B-profile: B-lines on one side, A-lines on the other.
  • C-profile: anterior consolidation.
  • PLAPS: consolidation and/or effusion at the posterolateral point.
Decision tree
  • B-profile → haemodynamic (cardiogenic) pulmonary oedema.
  • B'-profile, A/B-profile or C-profile → pneumonia.
  • A'-profile + lung point → pneumothorax. A'-profile without a lung point → no conclusion; needs other imaging.
  • A-profile → scan the leg veins (see DVT): DVT → pulmonary embolism; no DVT, PLAPS present → pneumonia (A-no-V-PLAPS); no DVT, no PLAPS ('nude profile') → COPD or asthma exacerbation.

Reported accuracy (Lichtenstein 2008), for example: B-profile for pulmonary oedema, sensitivity 97% and specificity 95%; A-profile plus DVT for PE, sensitivity 81% and specificity 99%.

Limits: one patient, one main diagnosis. The study excluded patients with rare or multiple diagnoses (41 of 301), the PE arm rested on 21 patients, and it was done by a highly expert operator. It does not cover heart, IVC or pleural fluid in detail; pair it with focused cardiac ultrasound.

12 · FALLS protocol

Fluid Administration Limited by Lung Sonography.

FALLS (Lichtenstein 2013) works through Weil's four shock types in order, using heart and lung ultrasound with the same simple probe.

1 · ObstructiveLook for pericardial effusion and tamponade, then a dilated RV (pulmonary embolism; check the leg veins), then abolished lung sliding (tension pneumothorax).
2 · Cardiogenic (left heart)A B-profile means pulmonary oedema. Do not give fluid.
3 · A-profile: fluid allowedNo left-sided pulmonary oedema, so fluid can be given. If the shock improves with fluid (a FALLS-responder), it was hypovolaemic.
4 · DistributiveIf shock persists despite fluid and B-lines start to appear (A → B transformation), stop the fluid. Persistent shock at that point is treated as distributive, usually septic.
Schematic
Why B-lines are the stop signal

Interstitial oedema shows on ultrasound before alveolar oedema or hypoxaemia, so B-lines appearing during fluid therapy mark the point to stop.

Limits
  • B-lines show lung water, not fluid responsiveness or cardiac output.
  • Pre-existing B-lines (fibrosis, ARDS, pneumonia) remove the stop signal.
  • The final step, B-lines appearing during fluid therapy, has been validated less than the earlier steps (Lichtenstein & Bar 2024).

For the full shock approach (pump, tank, pipes), go to RUSH.

13 · Lung ultrasound score

Twelve regions, 0 to 3, a number for aeration.

Score each of 12 regions (its worst pattern)
  • 0: normal aeration. A-lines, or ≤2 isolated B-lines.
  • 1: moderate loss of aeration. ≥3 well-separated B-lines.
  • 2: severe loss of aeration. Coalescent B-lines.
  • 3: complete loss of aeration. Consolidation (tissue-like).

Total: 0 (normal) to 36 (worst). Some groups use a modified version: score 1 when B-lines cover less than 50% of the pleural line and 2 when they cover more, or add points for subpleural consolidations. State which version you use, and use the same one every time.

Uses
  • Recruitment and PEEP (Bouhemad 2011, ARDS). The reaeration score after a PEEP change tracked recruited volume measured by pressure-volume curves: +8 or more corresponded to >600 mL recruited. Lung ultrasound cannot see hyperinflation, so it must not be the only tool for setting PEEP.
  • Weaning (Soummer 2012). After a successful spontaneous breathing trial, an LUS score below about 13 at the end of the trial predicted successful extubation; above about 17 predicted post-extubation distress. In between is a grey zone. Later studies gave varying cut-offs, so treat these as a guide, not a rule.
  • Following progress in ARDS, pneumonia or atelectasis day to day; under anaesthesia, measuring atelectasis before and after a recruitment manoeuvre.

The score measures aeration. It does not tell you why aeration is lost.

14 · In theatre and recovery

Perioperative lung ultrasound.

1 · Endobronchial intubation

The intubated lung slides. The non-ventilated lung shows no sliding but a lung pulse, followed later by absorption atelectasis. To check: put a linear probe at the upper BLUE point on each side during ventilation, and look for sliding on both sides.

One study found ultrasound (cuff and tracheal views plus bilateral sliding) separated tracheal from bronchial intubation with 93% sensitivity and 96% specificity, against 66% and 59% for auscultation (Ramsingh 2016). If sliding is present on one side only with a lung pulse on the other: withdraw the tube 1–2 cm and rescan. Use the same sign as an adjunct to confirm lung isolation; fibreoptic bronchoscopy remains the standard for double-lumen tube or blocker position. (Ramsingh: 42 patients, single centre; it does not replace capnography or bronchoscopy.)

2 · Atelectasis under anaesthesia

Posterior B-lines and dependent subpleural consolidations develop soon after induction, particularly with high FiO2, obesity, laparoscopy and steep Trendelenburg. A pilot study suggested LUS can track perioperative atelectasis (Monastesse 2017). Use them to show the effect of a recruitment manoeuvre or a PEEP change, and to assess hypoxia in recovery.

3 · Pneumothorax after a procedure

Subclavian or internal jugular line, supraclavicular or infraclavicular brachial plexus block, paravertebral or intercostal block, thoracic surgery or laparoscopy.

  • Ideally scan the anterior BLUE points on that side before and after the procedure.
  • Sliding or B-lines on the anterior chest after the procedure rule out a pneumothorax at those points, in the supine patient where air would collect.
  • Lung ultrasound does not cause the radiation exposure or delay of a CXR, but a CXR, or an ultrasound check such as the agitated-saline flush seen in the right atrium within 2 s, is still needed for line-tip position (see vascular access).
4 · Hypoxia or high airway pressure during surgery

Run a quick checklist: tube position (bilateral sliding), pneumothorax, new B-lines (fluid overload, TRALI, negative-pressure pulmonary oedema), atelectasis or consolidation, effusion.

15 · Pitfalls

No sliding is not the same as a pneumothorax.

Absent sliding without a pneumothorax
  • Apnoea, a pause in ventilation, very small tidal volumes, high-frequency oscillation
  • Endobronchial intubation or one-lung ventilation (the lung pulse is present)
  • Complete atelectasis or mainstem obstruction (mucus plug)
  • Pleural adhesions or pleurodesis, previous pleural surgery or infection, symphysis
  • Severe ARDS, pneumonia, fibrosis
  • Hyperinflation or emphysema (COPD, asthma) with small excursions

Reduced (not absent) sliding: phrenic nerve palsy, for example after interscalene block, reduces basal sliding. Confirm with diaphragm excursion or paradoxical movement on M-mode.

Mimics of a lung point
  • Large bullae can show no sliding and an edge that looks like a lung point; the history and CT help.
  • The heart border or a moving pericardium on the left (a 'pseudo-lung point'). Check the rhythm: it follows the heartbeat, not breathing.
  • Diaphragm and abdominal organs at the base.
Cannot interpret
  • Subcutaneous emphysema: E-lines and loss of the rib shadows hide the pleura. Press with the probe, or scan elsewhere.
  • Dressings, drains, obesity, wounds.
Artefact errors
  • Z-lines or E-lines mistaken for B-lines.
  • Harmonics and smoothing filters suppress B-lines or make the pleura look different.
  • Scanning a rib or the costal cartilage instead of an intercostal space. Find the bat sign first.
  • Isolated B-lines at the lateral bases can be normal.
Thinking errors
  • Absent lung point does not exclude a large pneumothorax.
  • An anechoic effusion can still be an exudate.
  • One profile, one cause: patients often have two problems.
  • Inconclusive is not negative.

    Scan deliberately

    Sliding, lines, tissue, fluid. Then the profile.

    1. Find the bat sign first: no pleural line, no interpretation.
    2. Sliding, B-lines or a lung pulse rule out pneumothorax at that point. Only a lung point proves one.
    3. ≥3 B-lines per space is positive. Diffuse means ≥2 positive regions on each side.
    4. Hepatisation and the shred sign mean consolidation. Dynamic air bronchograms favour pneumonia.
    5. Effusion: anechoic space plus the spine sign. Volume ≈ 20 × separation in mm.
    6. Use a protocol: BLUE for respiratory failure, FALLS or RUSH for shock, the LUS score to track aeration.
    7. Inconclusive is not negative.
