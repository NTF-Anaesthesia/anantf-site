POCUS masterclass · Trauma

eFAST.

Five windows, one question each: is there blood or air where it should not be, and does it change what happens in the next ten minutes?

Extended Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma. A bedside, binary, repeatable examination done during the primary survey. It answers four questions:

  • Is there free fluid (presumed blood) in the peritoneum?
  • Is there fluid in the pericardium?
  • Is there fluid in either pleural space (haemothorax)?
  • Is there a pneumothorax?

It does not grade organ injury, and it does not replace CT in the stable patient. Its power is in the unstable patient: it tells you which cavity is bleeding, so the team goes to the right place (theatre, angiography, chest drain, pericardiotomy) without leaving the resuscitation bay.

01 · Role in trauma

FAST is a triage tool for the unstable patient.

An ATLS adjunct to the primary survey, under circulation.

Where it earns its place
  • Unstable blunt trauma: a positive abdominal FAST in a patient who does not respond to resuscitation is an indication for laparotomy (or damage-control surgery) without CT. In hypotensive blunt trauma patients scanned by experienced surgeons, sensitivity and specificity were both 100% (Rozycki 1998): a best case from an expert centre, not a guarantee. Unstable here means a transient or non-responder to the initial fluid or blood bolus (ATLS).
  • Stable blunt trauma: a positive FAST speeds CT and surgical review; a negative FAST does not end the work-up. CT remains the reference for the stable patient.
  • Penetrating thoracoabdominal trauma: the pericardial view is the key window. Pericardial blood after a precordial or epigastric stab wound means surgical exploration (pericardial view sensitivity 100%, specificity 96.9% in 261 patients, Rozycki 1999; but see Ball 2009 on false negatives with a haemothorax). Abdominal FAST has low sensitivity here: a positive result is useful; a negative result rules out nothing.
  • Chest: ultrasound of the anterior chest is more sensitive for pneumothorax than a supine chest X-ray (Alrajhi 2012: sensitivity 90.9% vs 50.2%, specificity 98.2% vs 99.4%).
  • Serial scanning: repeat after any change in haemodynamics. An early negative scan may become positive as blood accumulates.
Cochrane (Stengel 2018, blunt thoracoabdominal trauma, 34 studies): sensitivity 74%, specificity 96% against CT or surgery. High specificity: rule in. Moderate sensitivity: do not rule out. Abdomen alone: sensitivity 68%, specificity 95%; chest injuries: 96%/99%. Children: sensitivity 63%, specificity 91% (fewer, smaller studies; wide confidence intervals).

02 · Limits

What eFAST cannot see.

A negative eFAST does not exclude
  • Retroperitoneal haemorrhage: pelvic fracture bleeding, renal injury, major vessel injury. Blood stays behind the peritoneum and never reaches Morison's pouch.
  • Hollow viscus and mesenteric injury: bowel perforation produces little free fluid early.
  • Solid organ injury without free fluid: a contained subcapsular haematoma or parenchymal laceration. The FAST looks for fluid, not organs.
  • Diaphragmatic injury.
  • Small volumes of haemoperitoneum below the detection threshold, especially early.
  • Clotted blood: clot is echogenic and can look like tissue.
And a positive scan does not tell you the fluid is blood: ascites, peritoneal dialysate, urine (bladder rupture), physiological pelvic fluid in women and pre-existing effusions all look anechoic.

04 · Where fluid goes

Blood runs downhill: dependent spaces and the probe map.

Supine, the most dependent spaces
  • Pelvis (rectovesical pouch in males; pouch of Douglas in females): the most dependent part of the pelvic peritoneal cavity; with Morison's pouch, one of the two main sumps in the supine patient. It detects the smallest volumes in DPL-infusion studies (Von Kuenssberg Jehle 2003: median about 100 mL), but in real trauma the RUQ, especially the caudal liver tip, is the view most often positive (Lobo 2017).
  • Hepatorenal recess (Morison's pouch): the most dependent upper abdominal space; the right paracolic gutter connects it to the pelvis.
  • Left upper quadrant: in positive FASTs with LUQ fluid, 84% had fluid in the left paracolic gutter, and fluid confined to the LUQ (6 of 100 positive scans) lay below the spleen tip and in the gutter, or in the subphrenic space above the spleen, never in the splenorenal recess alone (O'Brien 2015). The phrenicocolic ligament partly separates the left subphrenic space from the paracolic gutter, so blood can sit above the spleen without reaching the gutter, or the other way round; in O'Brien's series the gutter was the zone most often positive. Clear all of these zones, not just between spleen and kidney.
RUQ: find the liver tip

The caudal tip of the liver and the adjacent right paracolic gutter are the RUQ sub-zone most often positive, more than Morison's pouch proper (30 of 32 positive RUQ views; Lobo 2017). Always slide caudally to see the liver tip.

How much can you miss?

Detection thresholds vary by study and operator. For a supine Morison's pouch view the mean was about 600 mL in Branney 1995, though some patients were detected at much smaller volumes; the pelvic view detects smaller volumes. A negative FAST can coexist with several hundred mL of blood.

Position

Trendelenburg (head-down) moves fluid towards the upper abdomen and improves detection in Morison's pouch (Abrams 1999); reverse Trendelenburg favours the pelvis. Reposition only if the patient and spinal precautions allow.

    06 · LUQ, pelvis, pericardium

    Higher and further back on the left; scan the bladder before the catheter.

    Left upper quadrant
    • Probe more posterior and superior than on the right: posterior axillary line, about 6th–9th intercostal spaces, knuckles on the bed. The spleen is small and sits high and back; stomach and colon gas sit anteriorly.
    • Clear: left hemithorax (mirror image, spine sign), subphrenic space (missed by a probe too low and anterior), splenorenal recess, inferior tip of the spleen and left paracolic gutter (where isolated LUQ fluid was most often found; O'Brien 2015).
    Pelvis
    • Just above the pubic symphysis, angled caudally. Transverse and sagittal, fanning fully through the bladder.
    • Males: fluid in the rectovesical pouch, behind the bladder. Females: in the pouch of Douglas, behind the uterus (and it can lie between bladder and uterus).
    • A full bladder is the acoustic window: scan before urinary catheterisation, or clamp the catheter. Fill with saline only via an existing, correctly placed catheter, and not if bladder or urethral injury is suspected (pelvic fracture, haematuria, blood at the meatus): instilled fluid can leak through a ruptured bladder and mimic haemoperitoneum. Otherwise record the pelvis as indeterminate and move on; do not delay resuscitation.
    • A small amount of free pelvic fluid can be physiological in women of reproductive age; interpret in context.
    Pericardium
    • Subxiphoid: probe flat under the xiphisternum, aimed at the left shoulder, liver as the window; increase depth to see the posterior pericardium.
    • If subxiphoid is blocked (tenderness, gas, obesity, a wound) use the parasternal long axis (focused cardiac). There, pericardial fluid lies anterior to the descending aorta; pleural fluid lies posterior to it.
    • An isolated anterior echo-free space without posterior fluid is often epicardial fat.
    07 · Anterior chest

    Pneumothorax: look for sliding, B-lines and the lung point.

    Supine, air rises to the least dependent areas: scan the anterior chest from the 2nd intercostal space down to the anterior costophrenic recess (lower parasternal and paracardiac), then lateral zones, using several spaces on each side. Linear (or curvilinear) probe, sagittal across two ribs: the "bat sign".

    Pneumothorax is excluded at that point by any of
    • Lung sliding: shimmer of the pleural line with respiration.
    • B-lines: they arise from the pleural line, so the visceral pleura (lung) is touching the chest wall.
    • Lung pulse: the pleural line pulses with the heartbeat in an apnoeic or poorly ventilated lung.
    Pneumothorax is suggested by

    Absent sliding, absent B-lines and absent lung pulse, with A-lines remaining. M-mode: seashore (granular below the pleural line) becomes barcode (horizontal lines throughout).

    Lung point: where sliding lung meets the non-sliding pneumothorax. Specificity close to 100%, but absent in a total collapse.

    Absent sliding alone is not specific: apnoea, mainstem intubation, pleurodesis or adhesions, large bullae, ARDS, contusion with low compliance. Subcutaneous emphysema can make the pleura unviewable (indeterminate). More in lung ultrasound.

    08 · Pitfalls and mimics

    False positives, false negatives.

    False positives · anechoic but not haemoperitoneum
    • Perinephric fat: hypoechoic rim around the kidney, usually bilateral and symmetrical, does not track into the recess, has internal echoes.
    • Seminal vesicles: paired, symmetrical bow-tie structures behind the male bladder; confirm in sagittal. Fluid is angular and conforms to spaces.
    • Fluid-filled bowel: has a wall, contains debris and shows peristalsis.
    • Gallbladder, IVC, stomach, renal pelvis, ovarian cyst: contained structures with walls; fan to see they are round or tubular.
    • Ascites, dialysate, urine (bladder rupture): genuine fluid, but not blood. History matters.
    • Epicardial fat pad: anterior only, mildly echogenic, moves with the heart.
    • Stomach or pleural fluid confused with pericardial fluid.
    False negatives
    • Too early, too little: scan again.
    • Clotted blood is echogenic and can look like liver, spleen or bowel. Look for heterogeneous material in a dependent space.
    • Incomplete views: liver tip not seen, LUQ too low or anterior, empty bladder, no fanning.
    • Obesity, subcutaneous emphysema, bowel gas. Retroperitoneal bleeding (not visible by design).
    • Pericardial: a haemothorax may decompress a pericardial laceration into the pleura. A negative pericardial view with a large left haemothorax in penetrating chest trauma does not exclude cardiac injury.

    09 · Medical decision

    Haemodynamics decide what the scan means.

    Unstable · FAST +

    Haemorrhage control: laparotomy or damage-control surgery; activate massive transfusion; do not go to CT.

    Unstable · FAST −

    The bleeding is elsewhere or not yet visible: chest (haemothorax, tension pneumothorax); pericardium (tamponade); pelvis and retroperitoneum (binder, pelvic X-ray, angioembolisation or preperitoneal packing); long bones and external haemorrhage; non-haemorrhagic shock (tension, tamponade, spinal cord injury, cardiac contusion: RUSH). Repeat the FAST; consider diagnostic peritoneal aspirate or lavage if doubt remains and local practice supports it.

    Stable

    FAST positive: CT with contrast (non-operative management of solid organ injury may be possible); early surgical review. FAST negative: CT according to mechanism and examination; serial examination and repeat FAST. A negative FAST does not clear the abdomen.

    Pericardial fluid

    Assume haemopericardium: urgent surgical decompression. Pericardiocentesis is only a bridge where surgery is not immediately available.

    Pneumothorax

    Tension features: immediate decompression. Otherwise drain according to size, ventilation (positive pressure, transfer, theatre) and local guidance.

    Mechanism
    Haemodynamics ⓘ

    Unstable: persistent hypotension or ongoing transfusion requirement despite initial resuscitation (an ATLS transient or non-responder).

    10 · Serial scanning and documentation

    One scan is a snapshot. Bleeding is a film.

    • Repeat the eFAST after fluid or blood resuscitation, after any deterioration, after transfer, and before leaving the resuscitation bay if the first scan was early or negative.
    • Record each window as positive, negative or indeterminate, with time, operator and patient position.
    • Store clips, not stills, where possible.
    • Say what you did not see: "LUQ indeterminate: subphrenic space not visualised."
    • Know your limits: credentialed operators, and confirm with CT or surgery as indicated. The eFAST is a decision aid, not a diagnosis of organ injury.
    For anaesthesia, the same windows help in theatre and ICU: post-intubation pneumothorax, haemothorax after line insertion, unexplained intra-operative hypotension. Pair with focused cardiac and RUSH.
