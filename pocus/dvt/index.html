POCUS/DVT & vascular

POCUS masterclass · DVT & vascular

Squeeze the vein.

If the walls do not meet, the leg has a clot until proven otherwise.

Compression ultrasound (CUS) answers one binary question: does this vein collapse completely under gentle probe pressure?

  • Full coaptation (the walls touch) = no thrombus at that point.
  • Non-compressible vein = thrombus (acute or chronic) until proven otherwise. Echogenic material helps, but is not required.

Why it matters to us: postoperative and ICU patients are at high VTE risk. In the hypotensive or hypoxic patient, a non-compressible proximal vein plus a dilated RV changes the working diagnosis in minutes, at the bedside.

01 · Framework

Four questions, always in this order.

Use the same I-AIM framework as the other stations.

  • I
    IndicationWhat is the clinical question? Swollen leg? Unexplained hypoxia or shock with RV dilatation? A local protocol for high-risk delayed hip-fracture surgery? (Routine screening of asymptomatic patients is not recommended by CHEST, and sensitivity is lower without symptoms.) Would the answer change management? If not, do not scan.
  • A
    AcquisitionLinear probe, correct position, transverse views, compress at every landmark (and every 1–2 cm between landmarks if doing a continuous scan).
  • I
    InterpretationCompressible, non-compressible or inconclusive at each segment. Report the segment, not just "DVT yes/no".
  • M
    Medical decision-makingCombine with pre-test probability (Wells), D-dimer and the rest of the POCUS picture (RV, lungs). Treat, rescan in about 1 week, or refer for formal whole-leg duplex. Wells is validated in outpatients only: in inpatients and post-op/ICU patients use clinical gestalt, and a negative limited scan with ongoing suspicion needs an extended or whole-leg scan, or a repeat.

02 · Acquisition

Set up for success before you touch the leg.

Probe

High-frequency linear (roughly 5–12 MHz). Start depth about 4 cm; increase as needed. In obese or oedematous thighs, switch to a curvilinear (2–5 MHz) probe for the femoral vein, accepting lower resolution.

Preset

Venous or vascular preset if available. Set the gain so the normal lumen is black.

Orientation

Transverse (short-axis) for compression. Probe marker to the patient's right, as for other transverse views. Posterior views (popliteal fossa, calf) keep the same rule, so on the right leg lateral is on screen left and medial on screen right; on the left leg this flips. Longitudinal views are for Doppler and for following a thrombus tail, not for compression.

Operator

Screen in line with your eye and the leg. The non-scanning hand can provide counter-pressure behind the thigh at the adductor canal.

04 · Landmarks

Groin to knee: the five places to squeeze.

Follow the vein from the inguinal ligament downwards, compressing at each level. Tap a level to see it.

  • 1
    Common femoral vein (CFV)Just below the inguinal ligament, medial to the CFA.
  • 2
    Saphenofemoral junction (SFJ)The great saphenous vein (GSV) joins the CFV anteromedially: the "Mickey Mouse" sign (CFV = face, CFA and GSV = ears). ESVS 2021 suggests therapeutic anticoagulation for about 3 months be considered, as for DVT, when GSV thrombus is within 3 cm of the SFJ; refer for formal duplex.
  • 3
    CFV bifurcationA few centimetres below: the femoral vein (FV) and the deep femoral (profunda) vein, which dives posterolaterally. Compress just above and just below.
  • 4
    Femoral vein, mid-thigh to adductor canalDeep to the femoral artery under sartorius. In the adductor (Hunter's) canal it is deep and hard to compress: use counter-pressure behind the thigh, or a curvilinear probe.
  • 5
    Popliteal vein (PV)Superficial to the artery, from the top of the fossa down to the trifurcation (anterior tibial vein and tibioperoneal trunk; the trunk receives the posterior tibial and peroneal veins).
Say femoral vein, not "superficial femoral vein". It is a deep vein, and the old name has led clinicians to withhold anticoagulation.

05 · Protocols

2-point, 3-point, whole-leg: what each one actually covers.

Definitions vary between papers. State the one you used.

2-point CUS · commonest POCUS protocol

Point 1: CFV from the inguinal ligament through the SFJ to the femoral/deep femoral bifurcation. Point 2: popliteal vein from the popliteal crease (or upper fossa) to the trifurcation. Misses: femoral vein of the mid and distal thigh, iliac veins, calf veins.

3-point CUS

Adds the femoral vein in the mid-thigh (most commonly CFV/SFJ, FV mid-thigh, popliteal). Some authors use "3-point" to mean CFV + SFJ + popliteal: check the source. Pooled ED data show similar accuracy to 2-point (Lee 2019), but because isolated femoral-vein clots exist (Adhikari 2015), adding the mid-thigh FV costs little and closes a known gap.

Extended (full proximal) CUS

Continuous compression every 1–2 cm from the inguinal ligament to the trifurcation, including the first few cm of the deep femoral vein. The most complete proximal scan achievable at the bedside.

Whole-leg ultrasound (WLUS)

Proximal veins plus the calf veins (posterior tibial, peroneal, often muscular veins), usually with colour Doppler. Normally a formal vascular-lab study; detects isolated calf DVT.

06 · Protocols · simulation

Walk the leg. Where does each protocol look?

Most proximal DVTs involve the CFV or popliteal vein, which is why 2-point scanning works most of the time. A minority are isolated outside the two points (for example the mid-thigh femoral vein or the deep femoral vein): in one ED cohort, about 6% of DVTs were isolated to the femoral vein (5.5%) or deep femoral vein (0.8%), so 2-point scanning would have missed them (Adhikari 2015, single-centre, retrospective). Isolated calf DVT is not detected by any limited proximal protocol.

07 · Technique

How to compress properly.

  • Transverse only for compression. In long axis the probe slides off the vein and it can look compressed when it is not.
  • Straight down, perpendicular to the vessel, enough to collapse the vein and slightly deform the artery. Hold for a second, release, move 1–2 cm, repeat.
  • Full coaptation means the anterior and posterior walls touch along their whole width. Partial collapse is abnormal.
  • Keep the vein centred. If it slides sideways out of the beam as you push, it has not been tested. Angle slightly or reposition.
  • Freeze cines or split-screen "no compression | compression" images at each level for documentation.
  • Free-floating thrombus at the proximal tip: if you see a mobile clot, use gentle pressure only, document, and do not repeatedly compress.
  • Colour Doppler is an adjunct: filling defects, absent flow, absent augmentation. Do not use it to override a clearly non-compressible vein, and do not call a vein patent on colour alone if it does not compress.

08 · Evidence

What the evidence supports, and its limits.

Accuracy of point-of-care CUS

Emergency-physician CUS, meta-analysis (Pomero 2013; 16 mostly small studies, variable training and quality): pooled sensitivity about 96% and specificity about 97% against formal duplex for proximal DVT. Intensivist-performed CUS is also accurate (Kory 2011). Lee 2019 meta-analysis: 2-point and 3-point performed similarly (pooled sensitivity about 90% for each, specificity 95–98%). Confidence intervals were wide, and sensitivity was higher when an attending performed the scan and with DVT-specific training.

Strategies proven safe · symptomatic outpatients

Serial 2-point CUS plus D-dimer was equivalent to a single whole-leg colour Doppler study for 3-month VTE (Bernardi, JAMA 2008: roughly 0.9% vs 1.2%). After a single negative whole-leg study, 3-month VTE risk is about 0.6% (Johnson, JAMA 2010). ACCP 2012 and ASH 2018 accept either serial proximal CUS (repeat at about 1 week) or a single whole-leg study, combined with pre-test probability and D-dimer. The SRU 2018 consensus favours complete duplex (whole-leg, with bilateral CFV Doppler) as the standard diagnostic test. If only a limited proximal scan is done and is negative, SRU recommends repeating it in 5–7 days.

Isolated distal (calf) DVT

Not seen on proximal protocols. A minority propagate proximally (often quoted around 10–15%; estimates vary), mostly within 2 weeks. CHEST 2016, reaffirmed in the 2021 update: if not anticoagulated, serial imaging over 2 weeks; anticoagulate if severe symptoms or risk factors for extension, and anticoagulate if the thrombus extends (recommended if it extends proximally). CACTUS (Righini 2016): no significant benefit of LMWH in low-risk outpatients with calf DVT, but more bleeding.

Limits: evidence is mostly from symptomatic outpatients. Performance in ICU, post-op and asymptomatic screening populations is less well defined. Operator training matters.

    09 · Pitfalls

    Things that look like clot, and clot that looks like nothing.

    False positives · looks non-compressible, is not DVT

    Inadequate pressure

    The commonest cause. The artery has not deformed. Push more, use counter-pressure, re-centre.

    Lymph nodes in the groin

    Oval, hypoechoic cortex with a bright echogenic hilum. They do not continue as a tube when you slide the probe up and down; no paired artery; hilar flow only on colour.

    Baker (popliteal) cyst

    Anechoic, posteromedial, between the medial head of gastrocnemius and the semimembranosus tendon, often with a "neck" toward the joint; no Doppler flow. The popliteal vein is separate and compressible beside its artery. A ruptured cyst can mimic DVT clinically, and the two can coexist.

    Haematoma, abscess, muscle tear

    Not tubular, not paired with an artery.

    Chronic post-thrombotic change (not necessarily acute DVT)

    Real thrombus, but old: thick wall, bright synechiae, partial compressibility, often a small vein. Compare with any prior scan before calling it new.

    False negatives · clot present, scan called normal

    Acute anechoic thrombus

    Fresh clot can be as black as blood. Only compression reveals it. The vein is often distended.

    Duplicated femoral vein

    A common variant (estimates range widely, from about 1 in 10 to more than 1 in 3 limbs, depending on definition). One limb of the pair may be thrombosed while the other compresses. Look for two veins around the femoral artery.

    Obese or oedematous limb

    Inadequate penetration and compression at the adductor canal. Switch to curvilinear, use counter-pressure, or call it inconclusive.

    Wrong popliteal position

    A fully extended or hyperextended knee flattens the vein. Scan with the knee flexed 20–30°.

    Protocol gaps · iliac and IVC thrombus

    Mid-thigh FV, deep femoral, iliac and calf veins are outside limited protocols. With iliac or IVC clot the CFV compresses, but its Doppler loses respiratory phasicity and augmentation.

    11 · Perioperative & ICU

    The leg in the hypoxic, hypotensive patient.

    Where CUS fits in suspected PE

    ESC 2019: finding a proximal DVT on CUS in a patient with suspected PE is sufficient to justify anticoagulation without further imaging in many cases. A proximal DVT is found in roughly 30–50% of patients with PE, so a negative CUS does not exclude PE.

    In suspected high-risk PE with shock, bedside echo is the key test: if CTPA is not immediately feasible, RV dysfunction on echo is enough to justify reperfusion therapy. A non-compressible proximal vein adds weight. Multiorgan POCUS (heart, lungs, leg veins) improved diagnostic accuracy for PE in an emergency department study (Nazerian 2014).

    Terminology: the 2026 AHA/ACC guideline replaces "massive/submassive" with clinical categories A–E. Category E is PE with haemodynamic instability (from persistent hypotension or shock through to cardiac arrest). In category E the guideline supports reperfusion, with systemic thrombolysis or another advanced therapy (catheter-directed, mechanical or surgical), if bleeding risk allows. This corresponds broadly to ESC 2019 "high risk" (cardiac arrest, obstructive shock or persistent hypotension).

    RUSH "Pipes" · shock station

    Aorta plus leg veins. Aorta: an outer-wall-to-outer-wall diameter of 3 cm or more is an aneurysm; look for a dissection flap (covered at the shock station). A non-compressible CFV or popliteal vein in an undifferentiated shocked patient points toward obstructive shock from PE.

    Perioperative scenarios
    • Post-op day 2–10 sudden hypoxia or tachycardia, especially after major orthopaedic, pelvic, abdominal or cancer surgery.
    • Symptomatic leg, or a local protocol for high-risk delayed hip-fracture surgery (routine screening of asymptomatic patients is not recommended by CHEST): a pre-op DVT changes anticoagulation, regional anaesthesia timing and IVC filter discussions.
    • ICU patients with femoral CVCs: catheter-related thrombosis is common; scan around the catheter (compression may be limited by the line).
    • Upper limb (CVCs, PICCs): compress the internal jugular, axillary and brachial veins as for the leg. The subclavian vein cannot be compressed behind the clavicle, so use colour and Doppler there (loss of respiratory variation suggests central obstruction).

    The same vein–artery identification skills underpin US-guided femoral and peripheral access: vascular access.

    Echo signs of acute RV pressure overload · cardiac station
    • RV dilated: basal RV/LV diameter ratio > 1.0 in apical 4-chamber.
    • Septal flattening, "D-shaped" LV in parasternal short axis.
    • McConnell sign (RV free-wall hypokinesis with apical sparing), TAPSE < 16 mm, plethoric IVC.
    • A mobile right-heart thrombus (clot in transit) is a high-risk finding: treat urgently.
    • Lungs (lung station): often an A-line pattern with sliding. Hypoxia + dry lungs + big RV: think PE. PE may also show small peripheral subpleural consolidations (infarcts), typically posterobasal: an A-line profile is supportive but not required.
    • RV free wall thin (< 5 mm) in acute PE; a thick RV wall suggests chronic pulmonary hypertension.

    Close

    Squeeze, report the segment, know your gaps.

    • Linear probe, transverse, compress until the walls meet. The artery deforms first if you are pushing hard enough.
    • Non-compressible = thrombus (acute or chronic); correlate with history and any prior scan. Echogenicity is a bonus, not a requirement.
    • 2-point covers CFV/SFJ and popliteal; 3-point adds the mid-thigh femoral vein. Neither sees calf or iliac veins.
    • Negative limited scan plus a positive D-dimer or high pre-test probability: repeat in about a week or arrange a whole-leg study.
    • In the shocked or hypoxic patient, combine legs with heart and lungs: big RV + dry lungs + clot in the leg = PE until proven otherwise.
