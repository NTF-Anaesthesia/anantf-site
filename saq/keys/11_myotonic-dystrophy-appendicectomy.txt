A 30-year-old patient with myotonic dystrophy is scheduled for surgery for acute appendicitis.
Outline your anaesthetic considerations.
(Modified from FANZCA 2016A)

Examiner Report




Sample Answer
This is a young patient with myotonic dystrophy presenting for emergency abdominal surgery.
Myotonic dystrophy is an autosomal dominant myotonia disorder which poses significant anaesthetic
challenges, in particular cardiorespiratory management. Anaesthetic considerations include:

Patient Factors
• Airway
        o Aspiration risk due to weakness of the pharyngeal muscles from progressive bulbar palsy.
        o Evaluate for potential difficult airway (rescue bag-mask ventilation may be challenging)
                 ▪ Review previous anaesthesia charts (if available) for Cormack & Lehane grading
• Respiratory
        o May be associated with restrictive lung disease and OSA.
                 ▪ HD for post-op monitoring, KIV ventilatory support post-op in ICU.
                 ▪ Incentive spirometry if time permits.
        o Assessment of effort tolerance, ability to cough, oxygen saturations on room air, and
            recent spirometry results (if available) will be useful in determining disposition and need
            for ventilatory support post-op.
• Cardiovascular
        o May have cardiomyopathy, heart failure, arrhythmias, mitral valve prolapse (20%)
                 ▪ Review cardiac history (palpitation, syncope, angina, effort tolerance), 12-lead
                    ECG, 2DE (if available). Cardiology consult for optimisation if time permits.
                 ▪ Check for pacemaker/ ICD in-situ, and take peri-op precautions as necessary
                    (interrogation/ re-programming/ magnet placement/ defib pads).
                 • Defibrillator pads may be placed if high risk for malignant arrhythmias.
                 • Arrhythmias should be treated aggressively intra-op.
                 • Electrolyte abnormalities, in particular K and Mg, should be corrected pre-op.
                 • KIV 5-lead ECG, IA line may be required if cardiac concerns are present.
• Gastrointestinal
        o Delayed gastric emptying further contributes to increased aspiration risk
                 ▪ Use of PPI and antacid (sodium citrate) pre-operatively may minimise aspiration
                    pneumonitis
• Neurology
        o Behavioural and cognitive problems may be present, making counselling more difficult
            and patient cooperation (for awake procedures) more challenging.
        o Narcolepsy-like central hypersomnia/ central sleep apnoea increases sensitivity to opioids.
• Endocrinology
        o Associated with diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, and adrenal insufficiency.
                ▪   Endocrine referral for peri-operative management and optimization.
                ▪   May require stress dose steroids, with omeprazole cover.
•   Myotonia
       o Myotonic episodes may be triggered by a variety of causes during anaesthesia
              ▪ Offending drugs include succinylcholine, anti-cholinesterases, and opioids.
              ▪ Other triggers are hypothermia, shivering or surgical stimulation.
       o Care should be taken to avoid these triggers of myotonic episodes intra-op.
       o Anti-myotonic medications should be continued peri-operatively
•   Musculoskeletal
       o Care with intra-op positioning in view of contractures/myotonia to prevent pressure sores
           and neuropraxia.

Anaesthesia factors
• Multi-disciplinary team approach in managing this patient peri-operatively is essential to ensure
   safe and successful anaesthesia.
• Avoid sedative premedication such as benzodiazepines. Titrate depth of anaesthesia with BIS
   target 40-60.
• Care with laryngoscopy and jaw manipulation due to risk of temporomandibular joint dislocation
• Myotonic dystrophy not known to be associated with malignant hyperthermia.
       o Rocuronium preferred over Suxamethonium (known trigger for myotonia, hyperkalaemia)
       o Volatile anaesthetics may be used for maintenance.
• Discuss with surgeon on use of RA such as TAP block or local infiltrative techniques as part of
   opioid-sparing analgesia.
• Temperature monitoring, with use of passive and active warming devices such as Bair Hugger to
   maintain normothermia.
• Strict extubation criteria necessary due to risks of respiratory failure and aspiration
       o Pharyngeal weakness with poor airway protection
       o Altered gastric motility
       o Cardiopulmonary pathology
• TOF monitor with TOF ratio >0.9 may be used to guide muscle recovery, but care should be taken
   during use (may trigger myotonia)
• Use of neostigmine for reversal may cause myotonia
       o Sugammadex may be used for reversal (if Rocuronium was used for maintenance)
• Opioid-sparing analgesia regime should be continued for post-op pain. If opioid has to be initiated/
   continued post-operatively, then patient should be monitored in HD/ ICU.
• Standby phenytoin/ procainamide for myotonic crisis.

Surgical factors
• Limited time for optimization of patient’s condition due to emergent nature of surgery
        o Hydration may be maintained with IV fluids during NBM period
• Discussion with surgeons on surgical approach (laparoscopic vs open)
        o RA (Spinal/ Epidural) may be used for open approach
        o GA would be required for laparoscopic approach
• Acute abdomen (aspiration risk) necessitates RSI.
