Define frailty and discuss the role of prehabilitation for patients with frailty.(FANZCA 2019A)

Frailty is a clinical phenotype characterised by elderly patient with decreased physiological
reserves greater than expected from advanced age alone.

It was described by Fried et al in 2001 as the presence of 3 of the following:
• Unintentional weight loss (>4kg/year) or sarcopenia (low muscle mass)
• Weakness (poor grip strength)
• Impaired cardiopulmonary function
        o Self reported exhaustion
        o Slow walking speed
        o Low physical activity
Other assessment tools include Edmonton Frail Sale (EFS) and Frailty Index.

Prehabilitation is a multi-modal, multi-disciplinary team approach involving the geriatrician,
surgeon, anaesthetist, nurse educator, dietician, physiotherapist, psychologist, and patient to
enhance patient’s functional capacity to withstand a forthcoming stressor i.e major surgery.

It involves comprehensive geriatric assessment (CGA) with individualised intervention plan.
• Medical optimisation of patient’s pre-existing conditions, including identifying and
     correcting pre-op anaemia, smoking and alcohol cessation
• Nutritional support to correct sarcopenia by increasing protein (>1.5g/kg/day) and total
     caloric intake.
• Physical exercise consisting of (1) strength training, (2) aerobic exercise and (3) flexibility
     exercise. Aims to improve strength, cardiopulmonary fitness and flexibility.
• Psychological support to reduce stress/anxiety. Screening for cognitive impairment, and
     provision of cognitive prehabilitation.

Prehabilitation has the potential to benefit patients with frailty by:
• Allowing previously non-surgical candidates to be fit enough to be considered for surgery
• Integration into ERAS protocols to
      a. Reduce pre-op anxiety and improving patient satisfaction
      b. Improve post-op pain
      c. Shorten hospital length of stay
      d. Facilitate earlier recovery of post-op function and mobility

Limitations of prehabilitation include:
• Time requirement of programme, which typically range between 4-8 weeks. This may not
   be feasible for patients going for emergency or time-sensitive (e.g. cancer) surgeries.
• Resource intensive nature, requiring close coordination from multi-disciplinary teams and
   patient commitment. Patient with poor social support may find it difficult to comply with
   the protocol and multiple appointments.
• Despite emerging evidence, there is no consensus on the ideal prehabilitation strategy
   (type and duration of intervention).
• Current literature is inconsistent and heterogenous in terms of (i) surgery type and (ii)
   intervention implemented, which makes it challenging for widespread adoption of a
   standardised protocol which is cost-effective to both patients and hospitals.
