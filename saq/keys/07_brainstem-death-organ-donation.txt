Outline the clinical criteria for diagnosis of brainstem death. Outline the physiological implications
of brain stem death following SAH in a patient listed for organ donation.
(Modified from HKCA 2012, FANZCA 2016A)

Examiner Report
Sample Answer
There are several pre-conditions required before brainstem death certification.
1. Presence of objective proof (CT or MRI) of sufficient irreversible structural brain damage, leading
   to total and irreversible cessation of brainstem functions.
2. Neurological depression or coma secondary to CNS depressant drugs, sedative drugs,
   hypothermia, metabolic and endocrine causes must be excluded
        o Na 125-155, K 3-5.5, glucose 4-15, SBP>100, SpO2 >95%, T >35C, no clinical evidence of
            renal or hepatic failure
3. Presence of intact neuromuscular function

Examination should be done by 2 independent consultants not involved in direct patient care:

Physiological issues which may affect viability of organs for donation in a patient with brain stem
death following SAH include:
1. Impaired perfusion of vital organs
2. Hypothalamic dysfunction
3. Effects from raised intracranial pressure (from SAH)

Cardiovascular
• Compensatory hypertension may be seen initially secondary to Cushing’s Reflex to maintain
   cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP). This is usually temporary.
• Hypotension should be corrected promptly to maintain perfusion pressures to heart, liver and
   kidneys.
       o May be secondary to reduced cardiac contractility, peripheral vasodilatation (loss of
           function of vasomotor centre, DIVC), and fluid losses (diabetes insipidus).
       o Fluids and vasopressors should be started.
       o Anti-hypertensives are typically stopped unless persistent MAP > 95 mmHg.
       o Combined hormone replacement (thyroid hormones, glucocorticosteroids, and
           desmopressin) may be needed if there is persistent haemodynamic instability.
       o Invasive BP monitoring with SBP >100mmHg, MAP >70 mmHg
       o HR 60-120 BPM
• Cardiac arrhythmias should be treated aggressively
       o Tachy-arrhythmias may be treated with amiodarone and beta blockers.
       o Brady-arrhythmias (more likely than tachyarrhythmia) due to loss of vagal tone can be
           resistant to anticholinergic drugs, and may require treatment with adrenaline.
       o Electrolytes such as K+ and Mg2+ should be replaced if deficient.

Respiratory
• Neurogenic pulmonary oedema
       o Secondary to ‘catecholamine storm’ in response to raised ICP, and worsened by systemic
            inflammation following brain death.
       o Lung protective ventilation strategies with PEEP use to improve oxygenation while
            minimising barotrauma risk.
       o Avoid excessive fluid administration.
       o Use of IV Methylprednisolone (15mg/kg) to attenuate pulmonary oedema is controversial.
       o Pulmonary hypertension can occur following neurogenic pulmonary oedema. Maintain
            adequate oxygenation, normocapnia, normothermia.
• Higher risks of respiratory infections. 30deg head tilt to minimise aspiration risk, with continuation
   of PPI. Diligent aseptic tracheal suctioning should be done. Use of PEEP to reduce atelectasis.
       o Lowest FiO2 to keep SpO2 >95%, PaO2 >80 mmHg
       o PaCO2 35-50 mmHg
       o Ph 7.35 -7.45
       o Plateau pressure < 30 mmHg

Endocrine and Metabolic
• Hypothalamic dysfunction (and pituitary ischemia) are common following brain death, and
   typically leads to loss of posterior pituitary function (ADH production) and anterior pituitary
   function (TSH, ACTH production)
       o Neurogenic Diabetes Inspidus (DI)/ cerebral salt wasting should be treated by replacing
            fluid and electrolyte losses. DDVAP may be used.
       o Hypothyroidism may benefit from T3/T4 replacement, which may improve cardiac
            function.
       o Hypocortisolism may benefit from hydrocortisone replacement.
•   Hyperglycemia from insulin resistance and deficiency should be corrected to keep patient
    normoglycemic.
        o Serum glucose 4-10 mmol/L
•   Maintenance of normothermia with active warming devices may be required due to impaired
    thermoregulatory function of the hypothalamus (with hypothermia worsened by decreased
    metabolism, hypothyroidism, and vasodilation)
        o Core temperature 35-38C

Renal and Electrolytes
 • Renal perfusion may be impaired, and should be maintained with careful assessment of fluid
    status, and monitoring of urine output to avoid over-/under-hydration.
            o Urine output 1-2 ml/kg/h
            o Serum Na+ 130-150 mmol/L
            o Normal K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, PO4-
 • Electrolyte abnormalities (secondary to DI/use of hypertonic saline for treatment of raised ICP)
    are common, and should be corrected
     o Avoid hypernatremia (Na >155), which can lead to impaired graft function.
 • Nephrotoxic drugs should be stopped.

Liver
• Liver glycogen depletion may be minimised by maintaining adequate nutrition.
• Liver congestion may be minimised by avoiding excessive fluid administration, and maintaining
    normal CVP < 10 mmHg.
• Hepatotoxic drugs should be stopped.
• Similar to renal management, avoiding hypernatremia (Na >155) will help to reduce risk of
    impaired graft function.

Haemotological
• Coagulopathy from DIVC/thromboplastin release should be corrected
      o Target INR < 1.5, platelet count > 50 000 /mm3
      o Aim Hb >7 g/dL
• Thromboembolism risk is increased due to prolonged immobility. Use of compression stockings
   and intermittent calf compressive devices may be used for prevention.

Others
• Non-essential medications should be stopped.
• Preventive measures, early detection and prompt treatment of infections, including antibiotics
   administration, should be continued.
• Eye protection (eye tapes, eye drops) especially if cornea transplant is considered.
