1. Outline extubation strategies for anticipated difficult extubation of an adult. (ANZCA 2017A)

Examiner Report




An anticipated difficult extubation refers to a patient who may have
• Problems with maintaining airway
        o Poor neurological status
                ▪ Stroke, cerebral oedema, pre-op poor GCS
                ▪ Metabolic derangements (hypoglycaemia, hyponatremia, hypothermia)
        o Aspiration risk
                ▪ GERD, symptomatic hiatal hernia
• Problems achieving adequate ventilation and oxygenation
        o Neuromuscular disorders
                ▪ Myasthenia Gravis
                ▪ Suxamethonium apnoea
        o Poor respiratory status
                ▪ Brittle asthma, severe COPD/ restrictive lung disease
                ▪ Respiratory infection (pneumonia)
        o Poor cardiovascular status
                ▪ High inotropic support requirement, high arrhythmic load
• Potentially difficult re-intubation
        o Known difficult airway
        o Airway deterioration intra-op
                ▪ Major H&N surgery, airway trauma, RLN palsy, prolonged prone/ Trendelenburg
                     position, fluid overload

Taking reference from the UK Difficult Airway Society extubation guidelines for ‘at risk’ patient, I
would

Optimise patient factors
• Airway               Cuff leak test (>110ml drop in Tv) to exclude laryngeal oedema
                       IV Dexamethasone to reduce laryngeal oedema
                       Suction secretions from ETT/ oro-pharynx (in deep plane of anaesthesia)
                       Head position (left lateral head down if aspiration risk, head up if OSA/obese)
                       Bite-block/oral airway to avoid negative pressure oedema from spontaneous
                       inspiration against an obstructed (bitten) ETT
• Respi                Pre-oxygenate patient with 100% O2
                       Lung recruitment manoeuvre to minimise atelectasis
                       Spontaneous breathing trial with CPAP 5 cmH2O, ensuring regular breathing
                       pattern with adequate minute ventilation (>70ml/kg/min)
                       Consider inhaled B-agonist if concerns of bronchospasm
                       Consider Heliox/ inhaled adrenaline if concerns of upper airway obstruction
• Neuromuscular        Adequate neuromuscular reversal with TOF ratio > 0.9
• CNS                  Avoidance of excessive sedative medications, long-acting opioids which
                       may depress GCS
• CVS                  Ensure stable haemodynamics, not on inotropes
                       Ensure judicious intra-op fluid administration (avoid overload)
                       Optimise CCF/ pulmonary oedema
•   Metabolic           Check and correct acidosis, hypoglycaemia, hypocalcemia
•   Temperature         Maintain normothermia

Optimise anaesthetic factors
• Personnel            Experienced anaesthetist, and skilled help/ assistance to be present
• Environment          Consider transfer to Major OT/ ICU if patient in remote location
• Equipment            Equipment for re-intubation, including difficult airway trolley, to be
                       ready.

Extubation strategies include:
1. Awake extubation with minimal stimulation and activation of airway reflexes by:
         o Providing adequate analgesia
         o Avoidance of tactile stimulation during emergence
         o Use of Lignocaine/ Remifentanil infusion
         o Insertion of nasal airway (if the ability to maintain upper airway patency is a concern)
         o Transition to CPAP/BIPAP/HFNC post-extubation (if mechanical ventilatory support is
            needed)
         o Insertion of Cook Airway Exchange Catheter via ETT lumen, secured at the same ETT depth
            at lips (provides patent airway conduit, and facilitates intubation in a patient with known
            difficult intubation)
2. Alternative Strategy: Deep extubation with LMA exchange technique (Bailey Maneuvre)
         o Involves LMA insertion after extubating patient in deep plane after ensuring adequate
            spontaneous respiration.
         o To facilitate smooth extubation in a patient with known anticipated difficult extubation
3. Extubation should be postponed if further optimisation of patients’ factors is needed, and patient
    should be sent to ICU.
4. Tracheostomy, if prior discussion done with valid consent, for patient with long term risk factors
    for extubation failure.

Post-extubation care
• Frequent assessment and close monitoring for respiratory complications, with continuous SpO2,
   RR and ETCO2 monitoring.
• Equipment for re-intubation, including difficult airway trolley, should be present at all times in
   PACU.
• Adequate analgesia to facilitate lung mechanics, which needs to be balanced by minimisation of
   opioid related respiratory depression
• Plan for safe transfer, including supplemental O2, to High Dependency after discharge from PACU.
• Proper documentation/ handover/communication to PACU/ HD staff
