A 25-year-old woman who is 30 weeks pregnant has been referred to your tertiary high-risk obstetric
clinic. She has complex cyanotic congenital heart disease and now functions with a Fontan
circulation. How would you stratify the cardiovascular risk? What are the issues relevant to
anaesthetic care that will need to be managed for this patient?
(From ANZCA 2014B)

Examiner Report




Sample Answer
Answer provided here is aspirational, and not meant to be reproduced under exam conditions. In-
depth knowledge of subspecialisation (OBAN-Cardiac) is not expected for registrar level exam.

I would risk stratify patient by based on the modified WHO classification and CARPREG II scoring. I
would review patient’s history, physical examination, and investigations specifically looking out for
complications associated with Fontan circulation which will place patient at mWHO IV, as opposed to
mWHO III (well parturient post-Fontan with no complication).

Factors that increases cardiovascular risk for anaesthesia for this patient:
• History:
        o Poor effort tolerance (NYHA III-IV).
        o Cardiac symptoms (chest pain, SOB, PND, palpitations).
        o Presence of refractory arrhythmias.
• Physical Examination:
        o Poor SpO2 (<90%) on room air. o Presence of murmurs.
        o Bibasal crepitations. Palpable/loud P2. Raised JVP/peripheral oedema.
• Investigations:
        o TTE showing ventricular function (poor EF), valvular abnormalities (in particular,
            modsevere AV regurgitations) pulmonary hypertension, LVOT obstruction.
        o Evidence of protein-losing enteropathy.

This is a patient in third trimester with a complex cardiac condition and univentricular physiology
(Fontan), with high cardiovascular risk for anaesthesia.

She should be managed in a tertiary hospital with access to a multidisciplinary team of experienced
obstetricians, neonatologists, cardiologists, intensivists, and anaesthetists.
Issues relevant to anaesthetic care that will need to be managed:

Antepartum
• Maternal cardiovascular complications
       o Ideally, prior to her pregnancy, she should have already consulted a cardiologist and
          obstetrician for (i) optimisation of her heart condition and (ii) counselling on suitability for
          pregnancy/ labour.
       o Physiological changes of pregnancy (especially CVS and respi) can worsen her cardiac
          function, and patient should be followed up monthly with her cardiologist.
       o New symptoms such as disproportionate/ unexplained dyspnoea or new murmur should
          be investigated with TTE.
       o Any need for cardiac intervention (percutaneous or surgical) should be discussed with a
          multidisciplinary team.
       o Early delivery (foetus is viable at 30 weeks GA) if patient’s cardiac status deteriorates.
       o Risk of peri-partum cardiac failure, including death, should be communicated and
          counselled to patient.
• Maternal obstetric complications
       o Increased risk of pre-eclampsia and gestational DM compared to general population. They
          should be screened and treated.
       o Increased risk of thrombo-embolic complication, including intra-cardiac thrombus. Patient
          may need to be started on anti-coagulants, which should be balanced with bleeding risk
          and possibly central neuraxial use.
• Foetal complications
       o Teratogenicity risk especially with Warfarin during 1st trimester.
       o Foetus may be at higher risk of congenital heart disease and screening should be offered.
          If foetal congenital heart disease is detected, delivery should be at an institution that can
          provide neonatal cardiac care.
       o Higher risk of foetal growth restriction. Delaying delivery date may improve foetal survival
          rate.
• Delivery date (should be planned and discussed during antenatal visit)
       o Induction of labour is recommended at 40 weeks of gestation in women with cardiac
          disease, which reduces risk of Em LSCS and still births.
       o The duration of gestation should be balanced between adequate intra-uterine growth vs
          increasing cardiovascular risks to mother during pregnancy.
• Mode of delivery (should be planned and discussed during antenatal visit)
       o Vaginal delivery is recommended as first choice unless contraindicated.
       o Contingency planning, including Em LSCS if maternal cardiac status deteriorates, should
          be discussed with patient.

Intra-partum
• Antibiotic prophylaxis for IE.
• Anaesthesia/analgesia
        o Choice of CNB (epidural/spinal) vs GA should be discussed.
        o Anti-coagulant (if started) stop and (re)start date will need to be planned to facilitate CNB.
        o Patient is also at risk of pre-term delivery. Hence, patient should also be counselled for GA
           in the event of unplanned early labour requiring LSCS.
• Haemodynamic monitoring during delivery.
        o Continuous vitals monitoring, including telemetry, in delivery suite.
        o IA line and CVP if patient undergoes GA.
       o Having a 2nd (cardiac) anaesthetist who is proficient in TEE will be useful for GA for LSCS.
•   Optimisation of physiology in Fontan circulation during labour.
       o Fluid shifts/ autotransfusion and sympathetic stress from contractions during labour can
           worsen cardiac function, and should be anticipated and managed promptly.
       o Supplemental O2 to optimise oxygen delivery to minimise myocardial depression o
       o Maintain normal preload
               ▪ Difficult to assess fluid status clinically as patient may be oedematous.
               ▪ IV fluids should be given judiciously to maintain CO (pre-load dependent). Avoid
                    fluid overload, which can tip patient’s solitary ventricle into failure.
               ▪ Flotrac may be used to guide fluid management. Urinary catheter for urine output
                    monitoring.
       o Aggressive treatment of arrhythmias
               ▪ Fluid shifts during labour can to stretching of atrial wall and trigger arrhythmias.
               ▪ Atrial arrhythmias should be promptly treated promptly
               ▪ Electrolytes should be corrected and patient should be kept normothermia.
       o Maintain normal SVR
               ▪ Epidural analgesia should be slowly titrated to relief, to minimise hypotension
                    associated with systemic vasodilation.
               ▪ Vasopressors (phenylephrine) may be needed.
       o Avoid worsening pulmonary hypertension
               ▪ Achieved by minimising pain, hypoxemia, hypercardia, and acidosis.
       o Avoid air bubbles in IV infusion lines
               ▪ Especially if patient has residual left-to-right shunting (PDA/ASD/VSD)

Post-partum
• Disposition post-delivery
       o Patient should be monitored closely during the first 24-48h post-partum in the delivery
           suite or high dependency unit with continuous telemetry monitoring.
       o Early cardiology review to assess and optimise maternal cardiac status.
• Elevated PPH risk
       o Slow IV infusion of Oxytocin may be administered for uterine contraction. Avoid bolus.
       o Avoid Ergometrine (risk of worsening PVR)
• Elevated thrombo-embolic risk
       o Mechanical thrombo-embolic prophylaxis (compression stockings and calf pumps) should
           continue during and after labour, especially upon cessation of anti-coagulant.
       o Encourage early ambulation if patient is haemodynamically stable
