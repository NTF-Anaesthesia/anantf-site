  3. You are providing anaesthetic care for a 65 year old male, who weighs 98 kg, with a height
  of 170 cm. He has hypertension and is a heavy smoker (20 pack-years).
  (a) Outline the physiological response to the establishment of pneumoperitoneum and
      subsequent steep Trendelenberg positioning (50%).
  (b) You observe a steady increase in his end-tidal carbon dioxide tension, from 35 to 70
      mmHg, during the first hour of anaesthesia with an associated increase in heart rate to
      110 bpm. Describe your assessment (50%) and management (50%).

(a) Physiological responses arising from
Establishment of Pneumoperitoneum
CVS Higher IAP leading
                Initial autotransfusion (mechanical displacement) from splanchnic circulation
                Followed by impeded venous return, and drop in preload
        Increase in systemic vascular resistance (afterload)
        Vagal stimulation leading to bradycardia
        Hypercarbia leading to sympathetic stimulation with tachycardia/ arrhythmias and
        increased contractility, but may cause direct myocardial depression at higher PaCO2
        > 65mmHg
        Right shift of OHDC due to respiratory acidosis, increased O2 release at tissue level
Respi Diaphragmatic splinting, with increased airway pressures, risk of barotrauma
        Reduced FRC with shorter apnoeic time
        Hypercarbia (secondary to systemic absorption of CO2) leading to
            o Increased pulmonary vascular resistance
            o Increased respiratory drive, patient-ventilator dyssynchrony if not well
                paralysed
        Worsens atelectasis, V/Q mismatch
Renal Reduced renal perfusion, leading to lower urine output
GI      Impaired splanchnic circulation, leading to bowel oedema and post-op ileus
        Increased aspiration risk
CNS Raised ICP from cerebral vasodilation secondary to hypercarbia, impeded CSF venous
        drainage
Others Venous stasis (lower limbs) from compression of veins, potentially increasing DVT risk

Steep Trendelenburg Positioning
CVS Improve venous return and preload
Respi Further diaphragmatic splinting, with increased airway pressures.
       Further reducing FRC with shorter apnoeic time
       Further worsening atelectasis and V/Q mismatch
       Risk of one lung ventilation if inadvertent endobronchial intubation occurs
GI     Further increasing aspiration risk
CNS Further worsening ICP
Others Facial/ upper airway oedema secondary to venous congestion.
       Ocular perfusion pressure may also be reduced in prolonged steep Trendelenburg, risk
       peri-op visual loss from ischemia/ optic nerve injury.
(b)
With a steady increase in ETCO2 from 35 to 70mmHg with associated increased in HR to 110
BPM, I am concerned about:
• Hypoventilation from malpositioned ETT/ pneumoperitoneum/ steep Trendelenburg
    position, which might be made worse by patient’s obesity
• Inadequate analgesia/ anaesthetic depth/ paralysis with patient-ventilator desynchrony
• Pneumothorax, which could be contributed by patient’s underlying COPD
    (emphysematous lungs) in view of his heavy smoking history.
• Bronchospasm, which again could be contributed by possible underlying COPD
• Subcutaneous emphysema (surgical cause)
• Equipment failure (rebreathing, exhausted soda lime)
• Reflex tachycardia from hypovolaemia from surgical bleed
• Conditions associated with hypermetabolic state (malignant hyperthermia, thyroid storm,
    sepsis, pheochromocytoma)

I would inform surgeon to stop surgery. Deflate pneumoperitoneum. Level bed. Followed by
simultaneously assess and resuscitate patient according to ABC principles
Airway        Confirm correct ETT placement (check ETT depth marking at lips, no kinks in
              circuit/ETT)
              Check for high airway pressures/ obstructive pattern on capnography
              Manual bag-mask ventilation to check for blocked ETT/ poor lung compliance/
              chest wall rigidity
              Suction ETT if suspected secretions/ mucous plugging.
Breathing     Titrate FiO2/PEEP to maintain SpO2 >95%
              Auscultate bilateral lungs for equal breath sounds, to exclude bronchospasm,
              pneumothorax, endobronchial intubation
              Check that soda lime is not exhausted, insp/exp valves functioning properly
              Check ABG to correlate ETCO2 with PaCo2
Circulation   Check and ensure that rest of vital signs are normal. In particular, for
              hypotension (BP), arrhythmia/ ischaemia (ECG), hyperthermia (temp), and
              hypoxia (SpO2)
              Optimise anaesthesia depth, analgesia, and fluid resuscitation to correct
              tachycardia

I would see if ventilation improves, with resolution of hypercarbia and tachycardia.
• Once tachycardia and hypercarbia resolves, to restart surgery with gradual and gentle
    pneumoperitoneum and less steep Trendelenburg position
• If patient is still unable to tolerate pneumoperitoneum/steep Trendelenburg, to consider
    converting to open surgery (with optimisation of analgesia).
• Patient will need to be monitored for extended period in recovery room, to consider
    sending to High Dependency if there are any respiratory/ventilation concerns.

If there is persistent hypercarbia/ tachycardia/ hyperthermia/ muscle rigidity, to declare crisis
situation and manage patient as per Malignant Hyperthermia pathway.
