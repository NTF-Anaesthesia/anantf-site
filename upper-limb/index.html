Rapid fire

Eight things to know before any upper-limb block

  1. The higher the block, the closer the phrenic nerve. Interscalene weakens the diaphragm in almost everyone; axillary never does.
  2. Only blocks above the clavicle reach the shoulder (interscalene, superior trunk, sometimes supraclavicular), or the suprascapular and axillary nerves blocked directly.
  3. No brachial plexus block covers the cape or the inner upper arm: supraclavicular nerves (cervical plexus) and intercostobrachial (T2).
  4. Supraclavicular: corner pocket first, or the ulnar side (C8–T1) is missed.
  5. Axillary: block the musculocutaneous nerve separately, in coracobrachialis.
  6. Ask where the cuff goes. Upper-arm tourniquet: a plexus block plus intercostobrachial infiltration. No arm tourniquet: a wrist block or local infiltration may be enough.
  7. Document the nerves before you block after trauma: the radial nerve with humeral shaft fractures, the axillary nerve with shoulder dislocations.
  8. Count every milligram, rescue blocks and the surgeon’s infiltration included. A common ceiling for ropivacaine is about 3 mg/kg (lean body weight), lower in the frail.

What each block costs

Interscalene
Diaphragm (almost always), Horner’s, hoarseness.
Superior trunk
Diaphragm less often; not never.
Supraclavicular
Diaphragm in up to about a third; pneumothorax risk.
Infraclavicular, costoclavicular
Diaphragm usually spared. A deep target, vessels not compressible.
Axillary
No diaphragm, no pleura. Many veins: inject slowly.
Suprascapular, axillary nerve
No diaphragm (posterior approaches). Shoulder abduction weak.
Forearm and wrist
No arm weakness. Fingers still move with a wrist block.
Every arm block
A numb, weak arm: sling, protect it from heat and pressure.

Doses are ropivacaine for an adult, as used on the block pages. They are starting points: adjust for weight, age, frailty and every other local anaesthetic the patient receives.

Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. The neck and the axilla are full of vessels: a small intra-arterial dose near the vertebral artery can cause a seizure at once. Use colour Doppler, inject in small aliquots with aspiration, add up the milligrams (rescue blocks too), and know where the lipid emulsion is.
